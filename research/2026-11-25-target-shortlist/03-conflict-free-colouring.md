# Proper conflict-free five-colouring at maximum degree four

## Exact target and literature check

Caro, Petruševski and Škrekovski’s Conjecture 6.4 asks whether every finite connected simple graph of maximum degree $\Delta\ge 3$ has a proper conflict-free (PCF) colouring with at most $\Delta+1$ colours. In a PCF colouring every non-isolated vertex has a colour occurring exactly once in its open neighbourhood. The cycle $C_5$ needs five colours and explains both the degree threshold and the connectedness hypothesis at $\Delta=3$. The degree-three case follows from Liu and Yu’s theorem on superlinear list colouring and a direct PCF colouring of $K_{3,3}$. The original paper reports the degree-three case before posing the general conjecture. The equivalent degree-four target is PCF five-colourability for all graphs of maximum degree at most four. Graphs of maximum degree at most two have a square of maximum degree at most four, which can be greedily five-coloured. [Caro–Petruševski–Škrekovski, Discrete Mathematics 346 (2023), 113221, Conjecture 6.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.01088v1) · [Wang–Fang–Wang–Guo (2025), Theorems 1–2](https://www.sciengine.com/doi/10.1051/wujns/2025305453)

Liu and Reed explicitly leave $\Delta\ge 4$ open and prove the asymptotic bound $(1+o(1))\Delta$. Chuet, Dai, Ouyang and Pirot improve this to $\Delta+O(\log\Delta)$ for ordinary PCF colouring. Cho, Choi, Kwon and Park’s $2\Delta-1$ bound gives seven colours at $\Delta=4$. They prove the conjecture for chordal graphs. In a separate paper they give a five-colouring result for maximum average degree at most $20/7$, with an exception containing the 1-subdivision of $K_6$ that is impossible at maximum degree four. [Liu–Reed, RSA 66 (2025), e21285](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.02155v2) · [Chuet–Dai–Ouyang–Pirot, doi:10.1002/rsa.70054](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.04543v2) · [Cho–Choi–Kwon–Park, Discrete Mathematics 348 (2025), 114233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06125v2) · [Cho–Choi–Kwon–Park, Discrete Applied Mathematics 362 (2025), 34–42](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16390v2)

Kashima, Škrekovski and Xu strengthen the seven-colour bound to PCF $(\mathrm{degree}+3)$-choosability for $\Delta\le4$. Lu, Ying and Song’s stronger vertex-dependent list-size result covers subcubic graphs or graphs of maximum average degree below $12/5$, with $C_5$ excluded. Xu’s November 2026 result gives $(\mathrm{degree}+2)$-choosability below maximum average degree $8/3$, again excluding $C_5$. These bounds leave the ordinary five-colour degree-four case open in the papers checked. [Kashima–Škrekovski–Xu, Discrete Mathematics 349 (2026), 115330, Theorem 8](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20521v2) · [Lu–Ying–Song, arXiv:2608.29375](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29375) · [Xu, arXiv:2610.41668](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.41668)

The bounded November 25, 2026 search included arXiv title, abstract and full-text searches for the author names, conjecture number, Brooks-type PCF colouring, subquartic graphs and five colours, plus indexed Mathlib and Formal Conjectures searches. It turned up a general SAT solver but no formal proof or competing resolution. The repository’s checked main and research refs and the campaign’s GitHub issue and PR searches yielded no matching result or active duplicate. [Roch’s public PCF solver](https://github.com/sandro-roch/proper-conflict-free-coloring) · [Mathlib graph colouring](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Combinatorics/SimpleGraph/Coloring.html) · [Formal Conjectures](https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures)

The degree-four bound is sharp even among $K_4$-free 4-regular graphs. In $K_{2,2,2}$ the three parts have disjoint palettes. At most four colours can split at most one part into two colours, and some part then sees two monochromatic parts, with every neighbourhood colour occurring twice. Splitting exactly two parts gives a PCF five-colouring.

## Structural reductions

**Proposition.** If a PCF five-colouring obstruction of maximum degree at most four exists, every such obstruction of minimum order is 2-connected, 3-edge-connected, and $K_4$-free, with minimum degree at least three and maximum degree four.

Let $G$ be such a minimum-order obstruction. Each smaller graph of maximum degree at most four has a PCF colouring from one common palette of five colours. In particular, $G$ is connected, since colourings of its components combine. The degree-three result and the square argument in the literature check give $\Delta(G)=4$.

**One added neighbour.** Given the colours on at most three old neighbours of a vertex, at most one additional colour can make the new neighbourhood lack a singleton. If the old neighbourhood has at least two singleton colours, at least one survives. If it has exactly one, adding any other colour preserves it. If it has neighbours but no singleton, their colours are all equal, so adding any colour other than that common colour gives a singleton. If the old neighbourhood is empty, every additional colour gives a singleton. Any bad additional colour therefore occurs in the old neighbourhood.

**Minimum degree.** For a leaf $v$ with neighbour $u$, colour $G-v$. Extend to $v$ by avoiding the colour of $u$ and the at most one bad additional colour at $u$. For a degree-two vertex $v$ with neighbours $u,w$, colour the smaller graph formed by deleting $v$ and adding the edge $uw$ if absent. The colours of $u,w$ differ. Consider the original neighbourhoods of $u,w$ with $v$ omitted, removing the added edge in this calculation. Each has size at most three. Colour $v$ by avoiding the colours of $u,w$ and their at most one bad additional colour each. Both neighbours and $v$ then have a singleton. This uses the suppression move from Kashima–Škrekovski–Xu, Section 4, Claim 1. [Source](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20521v2)

**Cut vertices.** For a cut vertex $v$, colour each smaller piece induced by $v$ and one component of $G-v$. Relabel colours to give $v$ the same colour in each piece. Take a singleton colour at $v$ in one piece. Each other piece has at most three neighbours of $v$, so permute the four colours other than the colour of $v$ in that piece to avoid the chosen singleton on all its neighbours. These colourings combine into a PCF colouring of $G$. This proves 2-connectivity and rules out a bridge.

**Two-edge cuts.** Suppose a partition into nonempty vertex sets $A,B$ has exactly two crossing edges $x_iy_i$ with $x_i\in A$ and $y_i\in B$ for $i=1,2$. The two endpoints on each side are distinct. Otherwise that common endpoint would be an articulation vertex, unless its side consisted of just itself, contradicting minimum degree three. Independently colour the induced graphs. Let $a_i$ and $b_i$ be the colours of $x_i$ and $y_i$. Let $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ denote their possible bad additional colours, each different from the corresponding endpoint colour. A common permutation $\pi$ of the five colours on $B$ suffices if $\pi(b_i)$ avoids $a_i,\alpha_i$ and, when $\beta_i$ exists, $\pi(\beta_i)\ne a_i$.

For each distinct constrained colour on $B$, form the list of its possible images. If $b_1=b_2$ then its list has size at least one and each remaining list has size at least three, with at most three constrained colours. If $b_1\ne b_2$ then the endpoint lists have size at least two and any witness-only list has size at least three. If there are four constrained colours, both witness-only lists have size at least four because the two witness colours are distinct and neither equals an endpoint colour. These sizes give Hall’s condition for every number of constrained colours. Extend distinct representatives to a permutation. Relabelling $B$ with it yields a PCF colouring, which proves 3-edge-connectivity.

**A four-vertex clique.** Suppose $S$ induces $K_4$ and set $H=G-S$. Each vertex in $S$ has at most one exterior neighbour. Once $H$ is coloured, we seek four distinct colours on $S$. A vertex of $S$ then sees three different clique colours. Its possible single exterior neighbour may repeat one of those colours and still leaves a singleton.

First suppose some exterior vertex $x$ has at least two neighbours in $S$. Colour $H$ and at every non-isolated exterior vertex choose a singleton colour. For each vertex of $S$ remove from its list the colour of its exterior neighbour if any, and the chosen singleton at that exterior neighbour if one exists. Each list has size at least three. Hall’s condition fails only if all four lists equal the same three-set $P$. Otherwise use distinct representatives. An exterior vertex isolated in $H$ receives distinct colours on all its neighbours in $S$ and is also satisfied.

If all four lists equal $P$, the chosen singleton at $x$ exists and both it and the colour of $x$ are outside $P$. Give one neighbour of $x$ in $S$ its chosen singleton and assign the three colours of $P$ to the other clique vertices. All other exterior vertices retain their chosen singleton. If $x$ has at least three neighbours in $S$, then their distinct colours and the at most one old neighbour leave a singleton at $x$. If $x$ has two neighbours in $S$ and at most one old neighbour, there is again a singleton. If it has two old neighbours, their colours are different since $H$ was PCF-coloured. Assign the other neighbour of $x$ in $S$ a colour of $P$ different from the colour of the old neighbour that did not carry the chosen singleton. Thus $x$ is satisfied.

Now each exterior vertex has at most one neighbour in $S$. If any clique vertex has no exterior neighbour, colouring $H$ directly gives it a list of size five and gives all other vertices lists of size at least three, so Hall’s condition holds. Otherwise the four exterior neighbours are distinct. If they form a clique, colour $H$ directly. Their four distinct colours make it impossible that the lists are the same three-set, since this would mean all four lists omit the same two colours. If some exterior pair $x,y$ is nonadjacent, colour $H+xy$ instead. This smaller graph still has maximum degree at most four, as both endpoints each lost an edge to $S$. In constructing the lists exclude the colour of the exterior neighbour and its possible bad additional colour relative to its neighbourhood in $H$. If all four lists were the same three-set, then the bad additional colour at $x$ would be the colour of $y$, since the colours of $x,y$ differ. But in $H+xy$, adding $y$ with that colour already gives a singleton at $x$. Hence Hall’s condition holds and the four distinct colours extend the colouring. The resulting contradiction proves the proposition.

### Three-edge cuts

In a minimum-order obstruction, every three-edge cut isolates a vertex of degree three. For a cut with at least two vertices on each side, the crossing edges form a matching, a two-edge star together with a disjoint edge, or a three-edge path. A three-edge star would have its centre as a cut vertex, since its side contains another vertex.

For the matching, let a **compatible pair** on one side mean two terminals with distinct colours, each of which could accept the other terminal’s colour as an extra neighbour. The following palette facts are verified by [the cut checker](check_03_cut_permutations.js).

- Two sides with compatible pairs can be joined by permuting colours on one side.
- A side with a compatible pair can be joined to a side whose three terminal colours are distinct.
- When both sides have distinct terminal colours, a permutation works unless both sides have their three forbidden colours cyclically permuting their terminal colours.

If two terminals on a side are nonadjacent, add an edge between them before colouring the smaller graph. They each lost an edge across the cut, so this stays within the degree bound. Their colours provide a compatible pair relative to the neighbourhoods with the added edge removed. A terminal triangle already gives distinct colours.

If both sides have terminal triangles, arrange that one side’s forbidden colours are acyclic in the sense above. A triangle vertex has up to one further neighbour on its own side. It has no forbidden insertion if it has no such neighbour. If all three forbidden colours cyclically permute the triangle colours, all three further neighbours exist and their colours equal the other cyclic permutation of the triangle colours. Indeed each terminal sees two other triangle colours, and its forbidden colour is a singleton only when its further neighbour repeats the other colour. In particular the three further neighbours are distinct. When a further neighbour is absent or two coincide, colour the side directly. Otherwise colour the side after deleting the triangle, and give each triangle vertex a list excluding its further neighbour’s colour and its possible forbidden insertion colour. Each list has size at least three. Its further neighbour’s colour is excluded, so one list contains a colour outside the palette of the three further neighbours. Use that colour and give the other two triangle vertices distinct colours from their remaining lists. This maintains a singleton at all triangle vertices, since the two other triangle colours are different and there is just one further neighbour. The chosen colour prevents the forbidden-colour cycle.

For the star plus disjoint edge, colour the side containing the shared terminal directly and add a new vertex adjacent to the three terminals on the other side before colouring it. Each side initially has at least two vertices, so the augmented side has fewer vertices than $G$. The hub ensures its terminal colours are nonmonochromatic and there is a common colour that each terminal could accept from the other side. At a shared terminal, its old neighbourhood has size one or two because its cut degree is two. If it has size two its colours are distinct in a PCF colouring of the side. The cut checker verifies that these conditions allow a permutation. For the crossing path colour both sides directly. Each side again has one shared terminal whose old neighbourhood has size one or two, and the cut checker verifies a permutation for all such states.

The local checks range over all colour equality patterns of two or three terminals and all assignments of their possible forbidden colours or old-neighbour multisets. Independent palette renamings reduce the terminal patterns to the first-appearance labelings. The checks try every palette permutation and verify properness and singleton preservation at both sides of every crossing edge. The state sets include every realizable pattern and may include additional abstract states. The successful Node run tested 390,625 matching state pairs, including 170,565 for which the reduction requires a permutation, 45,800 star-plus-edge pairs and 10,000 path pairs. Reproduce it with `node check_03_cut_permutations.js`.

### Two-vertex cuts

A minimum-order obstruction is 3-connected. Suppose $\{x,y\}$ is a vertex cut. Each component of $G-\{x,y\}$ has at least two vertices by minimum degree three and therefore has at least four edges to $\{x,y\}$ by the edge-cut reductions. Since $d(x)+d(y)\le8$, there are exactly two components, both cut vertices are quartic and nonadjacent, and each component has four edges to them. Both cut vertices have a neighbour in each component by 2-connectivity. Thus the two attachment counts of a cut vertex are either $1,3$ or $2,2$, and $x,y$ have the same split type. Let the two smaller pieces each consist of a component together with $x,y$.

For the $1,3$ split, add $xy$ to both pieces before colouring. The augmented degrees are at most four. Relabel the two colours at $x,y$ to agree and choose a uniform permutation of the three other colours on one side. The vertex $x$ has one off-cut neighbour on the smaller attachment side, and its colour avoids both colours at $x,y$ because the augmented neighbourhood there has size two and a singleton. The three off-cut neighbours on the other side give at most one bad insertion colour. Thus the probability that $x$ fails after gluing is at most $1/3$. The same holds at $y$, so one permutation works at both.

For the $2,2$ split, first ask whether either unaugmented piece has a PCF colouring giving $x,y$ different colours. If so use such a colouring on that piece and colour the other with $xy$ added. After removing the added edge, at each cut vertex the unaugmented piece contributes two distinct colours. A monochromatic pair from the other piece is always permissible. A distinct pair fails only if the two unordered pairs are equal. With the two cut colours fixed and three free colours, the probability of equality under uniform permutation is at most $1/3$, whether or not either pair contains the opposite cut colour. Thus a permutation avoids both failures. If neither unaugmented piece allows different colours at $x,y$, colour both unaugmented pieces and align their common colour. Each pair is distinct and avoids that common colour, so there are six equally likely pairs under permutation of the four free colours and the failure probability for each cut vertex is $1/6$. This also gives a valid gluing.

### A sufficient condition for a matching four-edge cut

Suppose a four-edge cut is a matching. A colouring of one side is allowed to leave a terminal without a singleton in its old neighbourhood, provided all other vertices have their PCF properties. A terminal will receive exactly one crossing neighbour, so there is still at most one bad insertion colour. The cut can be joined if one side admits such a colouring with four different terminal colours and at most one bad insertion colour among the four terminals. This result uses the matching hypothesis throughout.

Write the terminal colours on that side as $a_i$, for $i=0,1,2,3$, and let $h$ be the colour outside their set. On the other side use boundary colours $b_i$ and possible forbidden colours $\beta_i$. To find a palette permutation, allow the image of each $b_i$ to avoid $a_i$ and the possible bad insertion colour at its opposite terminal. Also make the image of each present $\beta_i$ avoid $a_i$. Each $a_i$ is excluded from at most two lists before the one extra bad colour is excluded. Thus any three lists have union size at least four, and any four or five have union size five.

First suppose the sole extra exclusion, if it exists, is one of the $a_i$. Every list contains $h$. If two lists have union size one, all four $a_i$ are excluded from both. For every index $j$ whose colour $a_j$ is absent from the extra exclusion, the two labels $b_j,\beta_j$ must therefore be exactly the labels for those two lists. There are at least three such indices. If their terminals form a clique on the second side, their colours are distinct, which is impossible with just two labels. Otherwise add an edge between two of those terminals before colouring the second side. Their colours form a compatible pair relative to the graph with the added edge removed. This is impossible when their colours are distinct and each is the other’s forbidden colour. Hence Hall’s condition holds.

Now suppose the extra exclusion is $h$ and occurs at index $0$. If the second-side terminal at index $0$ has a nonadjacent terminal, add that edge before colouring. The row for $b_0$ is the only one that excludes $h$, and it cannot exclude the opposite colour of its compatible partner. All lists are nonempty. A two-list violation still requires both lists to exclude every $a_i$, since at least one contains $h$. This forces reciprocal forbidden colours at the compatible pair, again impossible.

It remains that the terminal $u$ at index $0$ on the second side is adjacent to all three other terminals. Its old degree is three, so those are its only old neighbours. Remove $u$ and colour the smaller side. Each remaining terminal $j$ then has one or two old neighbours, with different colours when there are two. Relabel the first-side colours so $a_0=0$, $a_j=j$ for $j=1,2,3$, and $h=4$. We seek a permuted colouring of the smaller side and a colour $p\in\{1,2,3\}$ for $u$. If the permuted colours at the other three terminals are $c_j$, require $c_j\ne j,p$ and a singleton in $(0,c_1,c_2,c_3)$. At terminal $j$ the two incoming colours are $j,p$. For a single old neighbour, PCF fails only if all three colours are equal. For an old pair $S_j$ of distinct colours, PCF fails only if $j\ne p$ and the permuted pair is $\{j,p\}$. The [cut checker](check_03_cut_permutations.js) verifies a successful choice for all 5,000 normalized triples of terminal colours and old neighbourhoods of either size.

There is also a direct argument when the three remaining terminals have two old neighbours each.

- If the three terminal colours on the smaller side are equal, map their common colour to $4$ and choose any $p$. The other four labels can be permuted uniformly. For each $j\ne p$, its old pair has probability at most $1/6$ of becoming $\{j,p\}$.
- If two are equal and the third different, map the repeated colour to $4$ and the singleton colour to $d\in\{1,2,3\}$ different from the index where it occurs. Choose $p\ne d$. The other three labels can be permuted uniformly. For each $j\ne p$ the probability of the blocked pair is at most $1/3$, whether it uses $d$ and a free colour or two free colours.
- If all three colours are distinct and some $S_r$ contains a terminal colour, choose $d\in\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{r\}$, and let $p$ be the remaining ordinary colour. Map the terminal colours to $0,4,d$, with $d$ assigned away from its own index. The two unused terminal labels map to $p,r$. At $r$ the blocked pair is impossible since its old pair includes a terminal label. At $d$ at most one of the two assignments makes the blocked pair, so choose the other. At $p$ there is no blocked pair.
- If all three colours are distinct and every $S_j$ avoids all three terminal labels, each $S_j$ equals the pair of unused labels. Map the three terminal colours to $4,d,e$ for distinct $d,e\in\{1,2,3\}$, assigning $d$ to index $e$ and $e$ to index $d$. Let $p$ be the remaining ordinary colour. Then the old pairs all map to $\{0,p\}$, which is permissible.

Each choice gives a singleton at $u$ and preserves properness across the matching. All other vertices preserve their singleton by the terminal restrictions or because their neighbourhoods are untouched.

Another sufficient condition is a colouring on one side with no forbidden insertion colour at any of its four terminals, and a terminal colour multiset different from a $2+2$ split. The case of four distinct colours was proved above. With three colours, two are used once, and the union of at least three permutation lists contains both of them and the two missing colours. The repeated colour can be excluded from at most four lists, so Hall also holds on five lists. With one or two terminal colours, add a hub adjacent to all four terminals of the other side and colour it. Its colour is unused among those terminal colours and their bad insertion colours, so at most four permutation rows are constrained. When the first-side terminal colours are all the same, the other four labels occur in every list. When there are two colours with multiplicities $3,1$, three labels occur in every list and the singleton colour cannot be excluded from every one of four rows. This again gives Hall’s condition.

More generally, let $m_c$ count the first-side terminals of colour $c$ and $w_c$ count terminals where $c$ is a bad insertion colour. There are at most $2m_c+w_c$ permutation rows excluding $c$. With the hub colouring on the second side, at most four rows are constrained. A sufficient condition is that, for each $k=1,2,3,4$, at least $k$ labels have $2m_c+w_c<k$. Those labels are present in the union of any $k$ rows. In particular, three distinct terminal colours and at most one bad insertion colour satisfy this condition. When the one bad colour is a missing label, both missing labels are present in the union of any two rows. When it is a singleton label, the other singleton label belongs to the union of any three rows.

### Degree-three vertices

The degree-three vertices of a minimum-order obstruction induce a graph of maximum degree one. Suppose a cubic vertex $v$ has cubic neighbours $a,b$, and call its third neighbour $c$. If any two of its neighbours are adjacent, colour $G-v$ and extend to $v$. Each of $a,b$ has two differently coloured old neighbours, so the only possible bad insertion colour is at $c$. At most four colours are excluded at $v$, and its own neighbours are nonmonochromatic.

Otherwise add $ab,ac$ to $G-v$ and colour this smaller graph. The vertex $a$ lost one edge and has original degree three, and $b,c$ each gain only one edge, so the maximum degree remains at most four. Write $A,B,C$ for the colours of $a,b,c$, and $f_a,f_b,f_c$ for their possible bad insertion colours computed in the original neighbourhoods with $v$ omitted. Then $A$ differs from $B,C$ by properness and from $f_b,f_c$ by the successful insertion of $a$ at $b,c$. If there is an available colour for $v$, use it, since its neighbours already have at least two colours.

If all five colours are excluded and $B=C$, all three bad insertion colours exist, are distinct, and are the three colours outside $\{A,B\}$. In particular, the two old neighbours of $a$ are both coloured $f_a$. The augmented neighbourhood at $a$ then has colours $f_a,f_a,B,B$, contradicting PCF. Thus $A,B,C$ are distinct. If $f_a$ exists, recolour $a$ to free $A$ for $v$. Otherwise all five exclusions force $f_b,f_c$ to be the two colours outside $\{A,B,C\}$, and we recolour $b$ to free $B$ for $v$.

In either recolouring, the moved vertex was cubic and its two old neighbours were monochromatic because it had a bad insertion colour. There are three candidate replacements after excluding its former colour and that common old-neighbour colour. At most one replacement is forbidden by each of the two old neighbours, and they are outside $N(v)$ since $a,b,c$ are independent. Choose a valid replacement after removing the added edges. The former colour remains permissible at the other two neighbours of $v$ by the exclusions above. It gives a singleton to the moved vertex and is proper at $v$. The other two neighbours retain distinct colours, ensuring a singleton at $v$.

Two adjacent cubic vertices with the same two neighbours outside the pair are also reducible. Delete them and colour the remaining graph. Choose two different colours for the deleted pair, avoiding the colours of their common neighbours. Each common neighbour has at most two old neighbours and can forbid at most one unordered pair of arriving colours, since the arriving colours are distinct. There are at least three available colours and hence at least three unordered pairs. Use an allowed pair. At each restored vertex the colour of its partner is a singleton.

If two cubic vertices $u,v$ lie in a triangle with a quartic vertex $w$, their respective neighbours $a,b$ outside the triangle must be distinct adjacent quartic vertices. The true-twin argument handles $a=b$. For distinct $a,b$, remove $u,v$ and add $ab$ if missing before colouring. The two remaining neighbours of $w$ get distinct colours $p,q$. When restoring $u,v$, each has at least two colours after avoiding the colour of $w$, the colour of its exterior neighbour, and that neighbour’s bad insertion colour relative to the graph without the added edge. The two restored colours must differ and should avoid the unordered pair $\{p,q\}$, to keep a singleton at $w$. Choices fail only if both lists equal $\{p,q\}$. The colours of $a,b$ are distinct and both outside $\{p,q,\phi(w)\}$. Hence the bad insertion colour at $a$ must equal the colour of $b$, and conversely. If $ab$ was added, inserting the colour of $b$ at $a$ in the augmented graph was already permissible, a contradiction. If $ab$ was present, each of $a,b$ must be quartic, as an original cubic vertex would have two old differently coloured neighbours in the coloured graph after deletion and would have no bad insertion colour.

### Algebraic approach

For quartic graphs, identify the palette with $0,e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4$ in $\mathbb F_{16}$, where the four nonzero elements form a basis over $\mathbb F_2$. A four-neighbour colour multiset has a singleton exactly when its sum is nonzero. Thus the product of $x_u+x_v$ over edges and of $\sum_{u\in N(v)}x_u$ over distinct neighbourhoods evaluates nonzero precisely on PCF colourings from that palette. A monomial with nonzero coefficient and every exponent at most four would prove existence by the Combinatorial Nullstellensatz. For three-neighbour multisets, the exact linear test is $t(x_1+x_2)+(x_1+x_3)$ with $t$ in an extension outside $\mathbb F_{16}$. The result is zero exactly for monochromatic triples.

The polynomial criterion is stronger than the target. For $K_5$ all fifteen nonzero linear forms in four independent differences appear as factors. Their product is the binary Dickson determinant whose terms include an eighth power. In characteristic two it vanishes modulo $(x_v^5\colon v\in V)$. Deduplicating neighbourhood factors matters. Without it, $K_{4,4}$ has fourth powers of the two neighbourhood factors. Squaring twice makes each a sum of fourth powers. A term $x_a^4x_b^4$ drawn from those two factors is killed by the edge factor $x_a+x_b$ in the same quotient. Removing repeated factors gives a nonzero truncated polynomial for this example. Exploratory coefficient computation found a nonzero truncated polynomial for all 2,312 labelled representatives of $K_4$-free quartic graphs through order ten. A general nonvanishing argument remains open.

There is a second polynomial with adjustable local factors in odd characteristic. For four neighbour variables and a partition into pairs $\{i,j\},\{k,l\}$, the quadratic

$$
(x_i-x_j)(x_i+x_j-x_k-x_l)
$$

vanishes on every bad tuple. A bad tuple with $x_i\ne x_j$ necessarily has the same unordered pair of values on $\{k,l\}$. For any good tuple in a five-element Sidon palette, some such quadratic is nonzero. For example $\{1,3,9,27,81\}$ is Sidon over the rationals and modulo 251, including sums using a repeated summand. Over the rationals a generic linear combination of these quadratics gives an exact local test. The space of quadratics is

$$
Q_A(x)=\sum_i A_i x_i^2-\sum_{i<j}(A_i+A_j)x_ix_j,\qquad \sum_i A_i=0.
$$

The product of the edge differences and one such quadratic per quartic vertex has degree $4n$ on a quartic graph. A nonzero coefficient of $\prod_v x_v^4$ therefore certifies a PCF five-colouring. It also certifies PCF colourability from arbitrary lists of size five, since the factors vanish on bad tuples with any numeric labels and the same coefficient can be computed over the rationals if it is nonzero modulo 251. In initial coefficient experiments, one parameter choice each gave a nonzero value modulo 251 for $K_5$, $K_{4,4}$, $K_{2,2,2}$, and four random quartic graphs of orders 8, 10, 11 and 12. This variant certifies cases where the undeduplicated characteristic-two product vanishes. A general coefficient bound remains open.

### Further checks and barriers

The five-colour bound already follows for line graphs of simple subcubic graphs from the adjacent vertex-distinguishing edge-colouring theorem. In a proper edge colouring, the colours on edges incident to each endpoint of an edge are distinct. The open-neighbourhood colours at the corresponding line-graph vertex contain a singleton precisely when the two endpoint colour sets differ. Isolated edges correspond to isolated line-graph vertices. [Balister–Győri–Lehel–Schelp, SIAM J. Discrete Math. 21 (2007), 237–250](https://doi.org/10.1137/S0895480102414107)

An acyclic colouring at the same palette size would be too strong even for $K_{4,4}$. An acyclic proper colouring of that graph uses four distinct colours on at least one shore, since two repeated colours on opposite shores would form a bicoloured $4$-cycle. Its shores have disjoint palettes. A PCF colouring needs at least two colours on the second shore, so the combination takes at least six.

The $3\times3$ rook graph $L(K_{3,3})$ defeats the strategy of splitting one colour class from a proper four-colouring. Suppose two colours $d,e$ in a PCF five-colouring could be merged properly. In the corresponding $3\times3$ edge-colour array for $K_{3,3}$, no row or column uses both $d,e$. If a row misses both then some column misses both, since they occur at most once in each row and column. That row and column share the same other three colours, which defeats adjacent vertex distinction. Therefore the $d,e$ entries form a perfect matching. Index them diagonally. For each of $d,e$ separately, consider the omitted ordinary colours in the rows and columns with that diagonal colour. The omitted-colour supports on rows and columns must be disjoint. If there are $k$ such positions for $d$, the $\ell_1$ difference in their omitted-colour histograms is $2k$. In the other group it is $2(3-k)$. The total histograms on the row and column sides agree because they count the same entries. Taking the larger group gives $k\ge2$ and $2k>2(3-k)$, a contradiction.

Even a bipartite graph may need both shores to use four colours. On each shore take the seven nonzero binary triples. Join $p$ to $\ell$ exactly when their dot product is one in $\mathbb F_2$. The seven neighbourhood incidence vectors on either shore are the nonzero elements of a binary space of dimension three. With at most three colours, assign them affinely independent labels in $\mathbb F_2^2$. Summing the labels on a neighbourhood is a linear map from that three-dimensional space to $\mathbb F_2^2$ and must vanish on a nonzero vector. This contradicts PCF at the corresponding quartic vertex. Yet a five-colouring exists. In binary-triple order $001,010,011,100,101,110,111$ use $(0,0,0,4,0,3,2)$ on one shore and $(1,1,4,1,1,2,3)$ on the other.

A seeded exploratory search obtained and checked five-colour witnesses for 7,200 sampled graphs on orders $18,24,30,42,60,90,120,180$. It sampled unions of disjoint-edge Hamilton cycles, graphs with matching edges deleted from those unions, and degree-preserving switches of chains of octahedral graphs. The solver used at most 202 search nodes per graph. This sampling covered selected graph families and supplied no evidence about unexamined instances beyond those families.

### A closed component of one-hole recolourings

A one-hole colouring is proper on its coloured vertices and has a singleton at each coloured vertex whose entire neighbourhood is coloured. A move fills the uncoloured vertex and uncolours exactly one other vertex. Even if the second vertex may be anywhere, a triangle-free 3-connected quartic graph can have a set of 30 valid one-hole states closed under all such moves, with no state admitting a direct full extension.

Construct a five-cycle with vertices indexed modulo five. Add two branches, each with four layers $A,B,C,D$ of five vertices with the same indices. In each branch attach cycle vertex $i$ to $A_i$, join $A_i$ to $B_i,B_{i+1},B_{i+2}$, join $B_i$ to $C_i$, and join $C_i$ to $D_i,D_{i+1},D_{i+2}$. Join matching indices of the two $D$ layers. Give the $A,B,C$ layers colours $0,1,2$, respectively, and give the two $D$ layers colours $3$ and $4$. All scaffold vertices whose neighbourhoods are full have a singleton whenever the filled cycle colours lie in $\{2,3,4\}$.

For any cycle hole, colour the remaining four vertices in cyclic order by $a,b,c,a$ for any permutation $(a,b,c)$ of $(2,3,4)$. The two cycle endpoints at the hole both have colour $a$. The two exterior neighbours of the hole both have colour $0$. The hole has a bad neighbourhood, so moving the hole to a vertex outside its neighbourhood cannot work. Moving to one of its exterior neighbours cannot work either. Filling with $0$ or $a$ violates properness, filling with $1$ destroys the singleton at the other exterior neighbour, and filling with $b$ or $c$ destroys a singleton at one of the cycle endpoints. Neither affected vertex is exempted by the exterior hole. A move within the cycle must again use $2,3,4$, since $0$ violates properness and $1$ destroys both exterior singletons. Properness of the remaining four-vertex path and the singleton conditions at its two internal vertices force another pattern of the form $a,b,c,a$. This proves closure. The graph itself has a PCF five-colouring, so the obstruction concerns only the permitted moves. The [checker](check_03_one_hole_barrier.js) gives the full colouring certificate and verifies 3-connectivity and closure by counting neighbour colours directly.

### Near-cliques and low-degree dominating sets

A smaller admissible fragment with two degree-three ports cannot force its ports to have equal colours. If they are nonadjacent, add the edge between them, colour the capped fragment by vertex minimality, and remove that edge. The interior constraints survive, and the ports have different colours. An equality constraint between ports would therefore have to depend on the colours supplied by their exterior neighbours.

The graph obtained from $K_5$ by deleting two disjoint edges has four ports. For any prescribed four exterior colours, this fragment can be coloured with distinct port colours. Choose the centre's colour $c$ to match the common exterior colour if the exterior colours all agree, and choose it arbitrarily otherwise. Each port can use any of the four colours other than $c$ except its exterior neighbour's colour. The four lists each have size at least three and their union has size four, so Hall's theorem gives distinct representatives. The centre sees four distinct colours, and each port sees three distinct internal-neighbour colours, enough to retain an internal singleton after its exterior colour is added.

There is also a sufficient condition via an independent set $S$ whose vertices have degree at most three. Assume every nonisolated vertex outside $S$ has a neighbour in $S$, and has either exactly one neighbour in $S$ or exactly one neighbour outside $S$. Reserve colour 5 for $S$. Start with $G-S$, and for each $s\in S$ with degree at least two, select a pair of neighbours and add the edge between them if absent. The auxiliary graph has maximum degree four because at each outside vertex every added incident edge uses one distinct deleted incidence to $S$.

Choose the pairs to minimize the number of $K_5$ components of the auxiliary graph. If $U$ is the vertex set of such a component, every vertex in $U$ has degree four in $G$ and each of its incidences with $S$ must have been used to add a distinct new edge within $U$. Its degree into $S$ was one or three. The number of edges from $U$ to $S$ is therefore odd. If all vertices of $S$ touching $U$ had degree at most two, each would contribute two incidences to $U$, a contradiction. Choose a degree-three $s$ touching $U$, with selected pair $uv$ in $U$, and let $w$ be its third neighbour. If $w$ were in $U$, its incidence with $s$ would be unused, which is impossible. Replace $uv$ by $uw$. Removing $uv$ breaks $U$, while the sole new edge crossing $U$ cannot belong to a new $K_5$. This contradicts the choice of the pairs.

Brooks' theorem now gives a proper four-colouring of the auxiliary graph. The chosen distinct neighbour pair gives a singleton at each $s$ of degree two or three. At an outside vertex the unique neighbour on its one-element side of the partition supplies a singleton. The argument requires the degree bound on $S$: a pair of different colours at a quartic centre still permits the $2+2$ pattern.

### Starting with few defects

Call a vertex defective in a proper colouring if its nonempty neighbourhood has no singleton. A vertex-minimal counterexample with a cubic vertex $v$ has a proper five-colouring with exactly one defect. Delete $v$ and colour the remainder. If $N(v)$ is monochromatic, a choice among the four proper colours for $v$ avoids the at most three insertion bans and leaves $v$ as the only defect. Otherwise $v$ is good and there are at least two proper colours, so one hits at most one of the three insertion bans.

A vertex-minimal quartic counterexample has a proper five-colouring with at most two defects. Again delete $v$ and colour the remainder. If its neighbourhood uses at most three colours and is good, a choice among at least two proper colours hits at most two of the four bans. If its neighbourhood is bad, it uses at most two colours, so a choice among at least three hits at most one ban, and $v$ is the other possible defect.

If its neighbours use four distinct colours, let $t$ be the remaining hue. If $t$ hits at most one ban, use it. Otherwise choose a neighbour $x$ that bans $t$ such that at most one other neighbour of $v$ bans the original hue $A$ of $x$. There are at least two choices for $x$, and at most two other bans that can equal either original hue. The colours of the three neighbours of $x$ in $G-v$ are $p,p,t$ for some hue $p$. At least two of the three original hues on $N(v)-x$ avoid $p,t$, so at least two hues $D$ are available to recolour $x$ while assigning $v$ the freed hue $A$. For each of the three neighbours $y$ of $x$ in $G-v$, at most one $D$ spoils $y$. If $y$ is outside $N(v)$, this is the ordinary insertion ban after removing $x$ from its neighbourhood. If $y$ is in $N(v)$, the new hue $A$ at $v$ replaces the previous $A$ from $x$, so $y$ sees its old $G-v$ neighbourhood multiset plus $D$. Choose a $D$ that hits at most one of these three bans. The pattern at $v$ is now $2+1+1$, $x$ is good, and at most one neighbour of $v$ outside $N(x)$ becomes defective through insertion of $A$. This proves the bound.

If a vertex-minimal quartic counterexample contains two triangles sharing an edge, one defect is enough. Take $v$ with a path $a,b,c$ in its neighbourhood and add $ac$ after deleting $v$, if that edge was absent. Use a proper conflict-free colouring of the capped graph, defining insertion bans using the real residual neighbours. The triangle $abc$ in the cap forces at least three distinct terminal colours. If there are three, the two real neighbours $a,c$ of $b$ have distinct colours. A missing terminal hue cannot be the insertion ban at $b$, since its real residual triple cannot have the form $p,p,t$ or $t,t,t$ for such a banned hue $t$. There are therefore at most three bans on the two available hues.

With four distinct terminal colours, the blocker recolouring from the preceding argument can cause two defects only when there are exactly two blockers $x_1,x_2$ of $t$, and the other terminals $y_1,y_2$ respectively ban the old colours of $x_1,x_2$. The two disjoint pairs $x_1y_1,x_2y_2$ cannot both avoid the triangle edges on $a,b,c$. If one pair is the surrogate $ac$, its ban contradicts the capped colouring. Otherwise one pair is a real edge, say $x_1y_1$. Recolour $x_1$ and insert its old hue at $v$ as above. The insertion at $y_1$ is then already accounted for among the three bans on the new hue, so at most one defect remains.

### Simultaneous Kempe swaps

For a proper colouring of a quartic graph, take an unordered colour pair $a,b$. Swapping these hues independently in any subset of their induced connected components preserves properness. At each vertex the two parity bits for $a,b$ toggle exactly when the vertex has an odd number of neighbours in the selected components. A vertex that has an odd occurrence of any other hue stays good. At the other vertices, keeping the same good or defective status amounts to one homogeneous equation over $\mathbb F_2$ in the component selection bits.

The sum of the ten numbers of components is at least $4n-m=2n$ because each vertex occurs in four pairs and each edge in one. A good vertex contributes at most one nonzero equation across the ten pairs, namely for its two odd hues if it has exactly two. A defective vertex contributes at most seven, since only the pairs meeting the at most two hues in its neighbourhood can matter. With $d$ defects, the sum of dimensions of the spaces preserving the entire defect set is at least $n-6d$. If this exceeds ten, some simultaneous pair swap is more than a global renaming. This count does not ensure that the swap touches a defect or changes the equations that block its repair.

## Graphs of pathwidth at most three

Every graph of maximum degree at most four and pathwidth at most three has a PCF five-colouring. The proof uses the structural reductions and an exact finite calculation of frontier states, with no bound on the number of vertices in the input graph.

Take a minimum-order counterexample within this pathwidth class. Pathwidth does not increase under minors, and every auxiliary graph needed for the minimum-degree, $K_4$, edge-cut and two-vertex-cut reductions is a minor of the graph being reduced. The induced pieces are immediate. Suppression contracts one incident edge. The surrogate edge in the $K_4$ argument is obtained from a path through the removed clique. In the three-edge matching cut, both sides are connected by 3-edge-connectivity, so the added intraterminal edge is obtained from a path through the other side. In the star-plus-edge cut, contracting the connected opposite side gives the added hub. In the two-vertex cut, both cut vertices meet the other connected component, so their added edge is obtained from a path through that component. The auxiliary graphs still have maximum degree at most four and fewer vertices. The reductions therefore show that the counterexample is $K_4$-free and 3-connected, with minimum degree at least three, with every edge cut separating at least two vertices on each side of size at least four.

Use a nice path decomposition, introducing or forgetting one vertex at a time with at most four active vertices. Once a vertex has been forgotten and some vertex remains to be introduced, a bag of size at most two would separate the two, contrary to 3-connectivity. Thus the first forget occurs with four active vertices. The first forgotten vertex must be adjacent to all three others by minimum degree. Until the last introduction, each next full step introduces a vertex into the three-vertex frontier and forgets one of the resulting four. For the last step, choose the first forget before finishing the remaining three vertices. At every step after the first, when an old frontier vertex is forgotten, the newly introduced vertex must be adjacent to it. Otherwise the new vertex has no edge to any forgotten vertex, and removing the two other frontier vertices separates it from the forgotten side.

A state stores the graph induced by the three active vertices, their degrees into the introduced graph, whether exactly one vertex has been forgotten, and the complete set of partial colouring signatures. A signature records each active vertex's colour and the counts of each colour among all its introduced neighbours. It comes from a proper colouring of the introduced graph that satisfies the singleton condition at every forgotten vertex. Canonicalize each signature under global palette permutation by naming active colours in order of first appearance, then ordering the remaining colours by their vectors of neighbour-colour counts across the frontier. These data suffice to add a vertex, try all its proper colours, update the counts, and forget a vertex only if it has a singleton. Subsequent vertices have no edges to a forgotten vertex. Thus the signature transition is exact.

The search starts with the seven possible active edge masks after the first forget, excluding the clique. It tries every attachment mask and legal forget at subsequent steps. It rejects a degree above four, a forgotten vertex of degree below three, and a new $K_4$. The edge cut of the forgotten set has size equal to the sum of the frontier's degrees minus twice its number of internal edges. A forgotten set of size at least two must have cut size at least four. A frontier vertex with degree already four has no neighbour to any unintroduced vertex, so it precludes further introductions by 3-connectivity. If the unused total degree capacity of the frontier is below three, no nonempty unintroduced set can meet the minimum cut bound. Once either restriction holds, the frontier must be final, which requires every active degree to be at least three. These are necessary conditions on a counterexample. Allowing additional states where the conditions alone do not guarantee 3-connectivity is harmless.

The [frontier checker](check_03_pathwidth_three.js) examines the reachable state graph to closure. It visits 5,145 states and 17,812 transitions. All 2,392 states with three active degrees at least three have a signature with a singleton at each active vertex. The largest signature set contains 793 elements. Such a signature extends the partial colouring to a PCF colouring of the completed graph. The absence of a bad terminal state contradicts minimality within the pathwidth class.

## Graphs of treewidth at most three

The frontier method also proves that every graph of maximum degree at most four and treewidth at most three has a PCF five-colouring. The prerequisite reductions above preserve the class because their auxiliary graphs are minors and treewidth is minor-monotone. The bound is sharp at pathwidth two for two five-cycles sharing one vertex. In each cycle, the edges and the degree-two vertices force the colour inequalities of $K_5$ minus the edge between the two neighbours of the shared vertex. With four colours those two neighbours must have the same colour. The shared degree-four vertex then sees only paired colour occurrences.

**Three-vertex boundaries.** Complete a minimum-order counterexample in the class to a 3-tree on the same vertex set. A 3-tree starts with a triangle and adds each new vertex along an existing triangle. For every such triangle, use an ordered three-vertex boundary. Each added pivot gives a four-vertex bag and creates three new triangles, one for each facet containing the pivot. Organize the construction below each triangle as follows.

- A piece with one pivot starts from its four-vertex bag, with the real edges from the original graph. Attach any pieces below its three new facets, then forget the pivot.
- Multiple pieces below the same triangle may be combined along that triangle.

Only the real edges are used in counting degrees and checking colourings. Distinct attached interiors have no edges between them, since all their cross edges in the 3-tree use the attachment triangle. A four-vertex bag can have any of the 63 proper subsets of the six possible edges, since the original graph is $K_4$-free. A boundary with a nonempty interior has at least one interior neighbour at each of its three vertices. For a piece with nonempty exterior this follows from 3-connectivity. For the entire rooted instance it follows from minimum degree three, since a boundary vertex has at most two neighbours within the boundary. For an interior of size at least two, its crossing edge count is at least four by the nontrivial cut reduction. Whenever a piece has a nonempty exterior, every boundary vertex also has an exterior neighbour by 3-connectivity. Therefore any piece used as a child in one of the two operations has boundary degrees at most three before the operation.

**Colouring transitions.** Use the same partial signatures as in the pathwidth calculation, with four rows temporarily during the pivot operation. Joining requires the colours on the shared triangle to agree. Try every palette permutation compatible with that agreement, add the neighbour counts, and count edges within the shared boundary just once. A vertex reaching degree four must already have a singleton, since it cannot gain any neighbour. Once this has been checked, its count vector can be forgotten. Check the pivot's full neighbourhood when projecting back to three boundary vertices. These transitions compute exactly the possible signatures of each piece, subject to the stated conditions.

**Finite representative sets.** A state is characterized by its ordered boundary edge mask, used degrees and signature set. In a pivot bag the state also records the last attached facet, and processes the possible facets in ascending order. For a fixed bag shape, replace a signature set by a subset whenever such a set is already represented. A colouring with the smaller signature set extends any way that a colouring with that signature can extend with the larger one.

On a three-vertex boundary, compare the sets of accepted exterior patterns instead. Such a pattern fixes the colour equality type of the three boundary vertices and the three multisets of colours to be supplied by exterior neighbours. For a used degree $d$ at a boundary vertex, the admissible multiset size ranges from $\max(0,3-d)$ to $4-d$ and its entries must avoid the vertex colour. A piece accepts the pattern if one of its signatures, up to a palette permutation fixing the three boundary colours, supplies a singleton at each boundary vertex after the additions. These patterns include every possible exterior colouring in a completed candidate and the option of no additions when degrees are already sufficient. A piece whose accepted pattern set is contained in that of another piece of the same shape is sufficient as its representative. Indeed any full colouring using the representative determines an exterior pattern that the original piece also accepts, so the entire exterior colouring can be retained while its interior is replaced.

The representative comparisons preserve the existence of an obstruction. Parse a proposed obstruction using the operations above and work up through the pieces. Whenever a new state has been discarded, substitute a represented piece of the same shape that is at least as restrictive. Any PCF colouring after substitution would yield one before it, so an obstruction remains uncolourable through the substitutions. The structural tests for the pieces depend only on boundary degrees and whether there is one interior vertex or more. That distinction is already determined by the boundary shape for any retained piece, since an interior with one vertex has exactly three crossing edges and any larger interior has at least four. Subsequent degree tests are therefore preserved. Substitution terminates with an uncolourable terminal state in the finite search, contrary to its verification.

The [treewidth checker](check_03_treewidth_three.js) processes reachable states with replacement and tries every compatible gluing operation between live representatives. For any final pair of live representatives, the later processed member triggers their gluing. A bag with no further available facet is projected immediately. The search checks a complete colouring at every generated three-vertex state with all boundary degrees at least three. It closes after processing 1,540 states, with 1,539 live representatives. It verifies 10,669 facet joins, 215 same-boundary joins, and 9,442 pivot projections. All 4,129 terminal tests pass. No bound on the original graph's order is used.

## Second-session barriers and an intermediate target

The second session (also November 25, 2026) did not find an unrestricted proof or a counterexample. It completed the order-13 census above and recorded the following rigorous tools and barriers, so that later attempts can avoid these routes or start from them.

### Splitting colour classes never creates a defect

**Lemma.** Let $c$ be a proper colouring and let $c'$ refine $c$, so every colour class of $c'$ lies inside a colour class of $c$. Every vertex with a singleton under $c$ has a singleton under $c'$.

*Proof.* If colour $a$ occurs exactly once in $N(v)$ under $c$, on $u$, then the $c'$-class of $u$ meets $N(v)$ only in $u$. $\square$

Consequently a PCF $(4+j)$-colouring can be sought by splitting $j$ classes of a proper four-colouring, and every defect of the four-colouring must contain a repeated colour from a split class. For a quartic defect with pattern $\{a,a,b,b\}$ the requirement is that the two neighbours in a split class receive different halves. For the pattern $\{a,a,a,a\}$ an odd number of them must move. These are $\mathbb F_2$-linear conditions on the indicator of the moved vertices. The $3\times3$ rook graph already shows that splitting one class of a four-colouring is insufficient, since that graph has no PCF five-colouring with two mergeable colours.

### Parity covers do not exist universally

For a quartic vertex, a multiset of four colours has a singleton exactly when some colour has odd multiplicity. Thus on 4-regular graphs PCF colourings and odd colourings coincide. This gives a sufficient condition. Let $G$ be 4-regular, suppose $S\subseteq V$ meets every open neighbourhood in an odd number of vertices, $G[S]$ is bipartite, and $G-S$ is 3-colourable. Colour $S$ from $\{4,5\}$ and $V\setminus S$ from $\{1,2,3\}$. The colouring is proper, and every vertex has an odd number of neighbours coloured 4 or 5, so one of these two colours is odd and the vertex is good.

The condition is not universal. Summing $|N(v)\cap S|$ over all vertices gives $4|S|$, which is even, so no such $S$ exists when $n$ is odd. For the octahedron $K_{2,2,2}$, writing $v'$ for the antipode of $v$, the equations read $Z-z_v-z_{v'}=1$ over $\mathbb F_2$, where $Z=\sum z$. Hence $z_v+z_{v'}=Z+1$ for all three antipodal pairs, and summing gives $Z=3(Z+1)=Z+1$, a contradiction. The closed-neighbourhood version $(A+I)z=\mathbf 1$ always has the solution $z=\mathbf 1$ on a 4-regular graph, so Sutner's existence theorem is satisfied trivially there. Nontrivial solutions are $\mathbf 1+K$ with $K\in\ker(A+I)$, and whether they exist depends on the graph.

### Lovász partitions with split palettes

A natural global route uses Lovász's partition $V=V_1\cup V_2$ minimising $\Phi=2e(V_1)+e(V_2)$, so that $\Delta(G[V_1])\le1$ and $\Delta(G[V_2])\le2$, and colours $V_1$ from $\{1,2\}$ and $V_2$ from $\{3,4,5\}$. Disjoint palettes make the colouring automatically proper across the parts, and most vertices are automatically good. The route fails already on eight vertices. The $K_4$-free 4-regular graph with adjacency lists

```text
1 3 5 6 | 0 2 5 7 | 1 4 5 6 | 0 4 6 7 | 2 3 6 7 | 0 1 2 7 | 0 2 3 4 | 1 3 4 5
```

for vertices $0,\dots,7$ has exactly ten partitions with these degree bounds. Six of them admit a compatible PCF colouring, but the unique $\Phi$-minimiser $V_1=\{0,2,7\}$ does not. The graph itself is PCF 5-colourable, for example by $(0,1,0,1,2,3,4,0)$. All claims are exhaustive and reproduced by [pcf-lovasz-partition.py](03-handoff/pcf-lovasz-partition.py). In an unpreserved scratch sample of 200 random quartic graphs with a locally $\Phi$-minimal partition, 6 failed in the same way and one search hit its node limit.

### Static witness schemes cannot certify even six colours

Fix for every vertex $w$ a neighbour $f(w)$, and require $N(w)\setminus\{f(w)\}$ to be rainbow. Every proper colouring of $H_f=G\cup\bigcup_w K\bigl(N(w)\setminus\{f(w)\}\bigr)$ is then PCF at every vertex of degree three or four. On a 4-regular graph of girth at least five the added pairs are non-edges of $G$, since there are no triangles. Each pair has at most one common neighbour, since there are no 4-cycles, so the $3n$ added pairs are distinct. Hence $|E(H_f)|=2n+3n=5n$. A 5-degenerate graph on $n\ge6$ vertices has at most $5n-15$ edges, so $H_f$ is not 5-degenerate. The same count holds when $f(w)$ is chosen as the last-coloured neighbour in a fixed vertex order. The numbers of earlier $H$-neighbours then sum to $5n$ and the first vertex has none, so some vertex has at least six earlier $H$-neighbours. Neither version gives a greedy certificate for six colours, let alone five.

### Reverse-BFS greedy colouring

Let $r$ be a root, colour the distance layers from the deepest to $L_1$ and then $r$, and at each vertex $y$ forbid the colours of its coloured neighbours and the at most one insertion ban of every neighbour $u$ whose last neighbour is $y$. Write $E(y)$ for the earlier neighbours.

**Lemma.** For $y\in L_d$ with $d\ge1$, every neighbour $u$ completed at $y$ lies in $L_{d+1}$, except that $u=r$ when $d=1$ and $y$ is the last-coloured vertex of $L_1$. Hence at most $2|E(y)|+1\le7$ colours are forbidden at $y$, and at most $2|E(y)|\le6$ unless $y$ completes $r$.

*Proof.* A neighbour $u\ne r$ coloured after $y$ lies in some $L_j$ and has a neighbour in $L_{j-1}$, which is coloured after all of $L_j$ and therefore after $y$. So $y$ is not its last neighbour. The same holds for an earlier neighbour in $L_d$, whose neighbour in $L_{d-1}$ follows $y$. Finally $y$ has a neighbour in $L_{d-1}$, so $|E(y)|\le3$. $\square$

With six colours a failure at a non-root vertex therefore requires $|E(y)|=3$ and at least two completed neighbours in the next layer. If $y$ does not complete $r$, it must be *critical*: one neighbour in the previous layer, three in the next, each of the latter completed at $y$ and carrying a distinct ban outside the three neighbour colours. Every vertex of a locally tree-like region is critical. In such a region one can instead keep each set $N(u)\setminus\{\mathrm{last}(u)\}$ rainbow, so no bans arise. A vertex then avoids its three children's colours and at most two colours of earlier siblings. That leaves $6-5=1$ colour, and zero with five colours. This tight count suggests that greedy arguments of this kind cannot reach five colours, although it is not a proof of that.

The heuristic with soft rainbow preferences in [pcf-bfs-greedy.py](03-handoff/pcf-bfs-greedy.py) is not a proof even for six colours. With seed 1, 1,000 random quartic graphs of orders 6 to 149 and six colours gave one failure. With seed 2 and five colours there were 345 failures. Over seeds 1 to 5 with six colours there were six failures, at a root, at a vertex completing the root, and at critical vertices in layers one to three. These runs are experiments only.

### Six colours as an intermediate target

The best published general bound at maximum degree four remains seven colours: Cho–Choi–Kwon–Park's $2\Delta-1$ and Kashima–Škrekovski–Xu's $(\mathrm{degree}+3)$-choosability. Kashima–Škrekovski–Xu ask whether every connected graph other than $C_5$ is PCF $(\mathrm{degree}+2)$-choosable, which would give six. Wang–Zhang confirm that for $K_4$-minor-free and outer-1-planar graphs of maximum degree at most four. A proof that every graph of maximum degree at most four is PCF 6-colourable would improve the general bound. It remains open in this work. The barriers above show that it needs either a global argument or dynamic control of the insertion bans. [Wang–Zhang, arXiv:2512.22805](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.22805)

### Two further six-colour routes

**Splitting two classes of a four-colouring.** By the splitting lemma, a PCF 6-colouring exists whenever some proper four-colouring has two classes whose splitting repairs every defect. In [pcf-two-class-split.py](03-handoff/pcf-two-class-split.py), seed 1 sampled ten random proper four-colourings of each of 100 random quartic graphs of orders 8 to 39. Every graph had a splittable colouring, and on average about 89% of the sampled colourings were splittable for some pair of classes. This suggests the stronger statement that every graph of maximum degree at most four has a PCF 6-colouring refining a proper four-colouring. It is evidence only. A proof would have to choose the four-colouring so that the $\mathbb F_2$ constraint system at the defects, mainly "the two neighbours of the repeated split colour move differently", is consistent.

**The Kashima–Škrekovski–Xu cycle reduction with six colours.** Their $(\mathrm{degree}+3)$ proof deletes a shortest cycle $C$ of length $k\ge5$. It colours $G-V(C)$ after joining the two outside neighbours $u_i,w_i$ of each cycle vertex, then colours $C$ from the remaining lists. With six colours this argument goes through in part. Joining $u_iw_i$ keeps the maximum degree at most four, since at girth at least five each outside vertex has exactly one cycle neighbour. It also forces $c(u_i)\ne c(w_i)$, and the insertion bans at $u_i,w_i$ avoid both outside colours. A quartic cycle vertex then keeps a list of at least $6-4=2$ colours. It needs a proper cycle colouring in which, for each $i$, the pair $\{c(v_{i-1}),c(v_{i+1})\}$ differs from $\{c(u_i),c(w_i)\}$. This abstract two-list problem is often unsolvable. For $k=5$ take lists $\{0,2\}$ on $v_0,\dots,v_3$ and $\{1,5\}$ on $v_4$, with blocked pair $\{0,2\}$ at $v_4$. The path $v_0\dots v_3$ must alternate, so $c(v_3)\ne c(v_0)$ and the pair at $v_4$ is always $\{0,2\}$. Random sampling in [pcf-cycle-two-lists.py](03-handoff/pcf-cycle-two-lists.py) finds unsolvable instances for both cycle parities, not only odd cycles with identical lists. A six-colour proof along these lines must use realisability of the lists or further recolouring outside the cycle. It must also replace the triangle and 4-cycle reductions of that paper, which used the larger lists.

### Kempe reconfiguration at order 11

A Rust port of the first session's reconfiguration experiment is [pcf-kempe.rs](03-handoff/pcf-census-rs/src/bin/pcf-kempe.rs). It reproduced the first-session table for orders 6 to 10 exactly, both the state counts and the maximal distance two. At order 11 it processed all 18,577 labelled representatives of connected $K_4$-free 4-regular graphs. These carry 15,358,623 one-defect states and 12,086,773 two-defect states up to palette permutation. Every one-defect state reaches a PCF colouring through simultaneous Kempe swaps without leaving the one-defect space, and every state with at most two defects does so within the at-most-two-defect space. The maximal distance, however, is **three** in both cases, so the two-move pattern observed through order 10 does not persist. An explicit witness, rechecked independently by [pcf-kempe-distance3.py](03-handoff/pcf-kempe-distance3.py), is the graph with adjacency lists

```text
1 2 3 4 | 0 2 5 6 | 0 1 7 8 | 0 4 5 7 | 0 3 6 8 | 1 3 9 10 | 1 4 9 10 | 2 3 9 10 | 2 4 9 10 | 5 6 7 8 | 5 6 7 8
```

with the one-defect colouring $(0,1,2,3,2,0,0,4,0,2,2)$. Any defect-repair theorem by bounded Kempe sequences must therefore allow at least three moves. The experiment remains consistent with the qualitative statement that low-defect states are Kempe-connected to PCF colourings. It is finite evidence, not a proof, and the witness contains the true twins 9 and 10.
