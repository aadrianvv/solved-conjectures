# Revised Local Irregularity Conjecture

## Eligibility and statement

Sedlar and Škrekovski's revised conjecture concerns a finite connected simple graph $G$ that admits a locally irregular edge coloring. For a color $c$, let $d_c(v)$ count the edges of color $c$ incident to $v$. Every edge $uv$ of color $c$ must satisfy $d_c(u)\ne d_c(v)$. The conjecture says that three colors suffice unless $G\cong B$, where $B$ consists of an edge $uv$ and two triangles rooted at each of $u,v$, with all other vertices distinct. This is the 10-vertex, 13-edge bow-tie cactus. Isolated vertices in color classes impose no condition. The decomposability assumption excludes odd-length paths, odd cycles, and the recursively defined exceptional triangle cacti.

The 2021 paper gives $B$ as a counterexample to the original conjecture and asks about further examples. The exact revised statement appears as Conjecture 27 of the 2022 preprint and as Conjecture 3 of the 2026 paper on total colorings. The latter gives the bound 220 for general decomposable graphs. The cactus case of the revised conjecture is proved by Sedlar and Škrekovski. The 2024 paper on 2-multigraphs gives related results for doubled edges and its own restricted classes. The subcubic computation in Lužar et al. addresses cubic girth classes and the restriction to two colors. Broader searches by the conjecture name, author, exact statement and bow-tie exception found no full resolution. MathDB lists the revised statement as open. ## Degree-two paths

Take a path of length $L\ge2$ whose internal vertices all have degree two in $G$. Write $c_i$ for the color of edge $i$ and $a_i=1$ when $c_i=c_{i+1}$, otherwise $a_i=0$. The irregularity condition on an edge between internal vertices is $a_{i-1}+a_i=1$. Thus the $a_i$ alternate. The monochromatic degrees at the first and last internal vertices for the endpoint edges are $1+a_1$ and $1+a_{L-1}$. A sequence of runs of equal edge color can realize prescribed endpoint colors precisely when the runs admit a proper path coloring with those endpoints. One run requires equal endpoint colors, two require distinct colors, and three or more allow either relation with three available colors.

This gives the following exact conditions for a connector between hubs. Let $s,t$ be the colors of the end edges and let $x,y$ be their respective monochromatic degrees at the hubs. A pair of internal degrees is available as indicated, and it must differ coordinatewise from $(x,y)$.

| Connector length | Available pair of internal degrees | Endpoint colors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The edge requires $x\ne y$ | $s=t$ |
| 2 | $(1,1)$ | $s\ne t$ |
| 2 | $(2,2)$ | $s=t$ |
| 3 | $(1,2)$ or $(2,1)$ | $s\ne t$ |
| 4 | $(1,1)$ | Either |
| 4 | $(2,2)$ | $s\ne t$ |
| Odd, at least 5 | $(1,2)$ or $(2,1)$ | Either |
| Even, at least 6 | $(1,1)$ or $(2,2)$ | Either |

The local description remains valid if both ends are the same hub and the connector is a private cycle. A pendant path, with its far endpoint a leaf, forbids hub monochromatic degree one for odd length and two for even length. Hence shortening a connector of length at least seven by two preserves existence of a locally irregular 3-coloring. The replacement still has distinct internal vertices and preserves whether the graph is a cactus.

## Three colors for separated branching vertices

**Theorem.** A connected noncactus simple graph has a locally irregular 3-edge coloring if its vertices of degree at least three form an independent set.

**Proof.** Call vertices of degree at least three hubs. The absence of adjacent hubs means each hub-to-hub connector has length at least two. The rest of the graph, after splitting at hubs, consists of pendant paths and private cycles, each attached to one hub. The hub skeleton is connected and has at least two vertices, since otherwise every cycle is private and the graph is a cactus.

For a hub $v$, put $b(v)=d_G(v)-t(v)$, where $t(v)$ is its number of private triangles. Reserve one alternate-colored spoke per private triangle. A hub with $b(v)<3$ must have exactly one hub-to-hub connector and one private triangle. Its total degree is three. For any given connector color, the triangle can be colored so that this color has degree two at the hub. Use the connector color on the light spoke of the triangle, another color on the heavy spoke, and the heavy color on the edge between their endpoints. This yields color-degrees one and two respectively at the two triangle vertices, with distinct color-degrees at each monochromatic adjacency. Consequently a connector ending at this pendant triangle can be treated as a pendant constraint at its other hub. The triangle end requires internal degree one on the last edge. Alternation along the connector prescribes internal degree one at the other end for even length and two for odd length. The far hub color can be chosen to match the endpoint relation in the path table. A connector cannot have such a hub at both ends, since then the whole graph would be a cactus. Remove these hubs from the skeleton and retain their pendant constraints on the remaining hubs. This preserves the prescribed count of spokes at each remaining hub.

The remaining hubs have $b(v)\ge3$. Assign each a main color. Of the remaining connectors, the length-three connectors are the only ones that inherently require distinct end colors. Call these the demand graph, retaining any parallel connectors. First properly color the underlying simple demand graph induced by the hubs with $b(v)=3$. Its maximum degree is at most three, so Brooks's theorem allows three colors unless it contains a $K_4$ component. If it contains one, each of its hubs uses all three spokes on the component, so connectedness implies the original graph is $K_4$ with every edge replaced by a length-three path. In that case give two hubs the same main color and give the other two distinct colors. Recolor one spoke on the connector between the same-colored hubs. Every connector now has distinct colors and a hub with monochromatic degree three. The length-three row of the table yields the result.

In all other cases fix the colors already assigned to the $b(v)=3$ hubs. Extend the labels to the other hubs to maximize the number of demand connectors whose ends have distinct labels. At an unfixed hub $v$, the number of same-label demand spokes is at most one third of its demand degree. Otherwise switching its label would increase the number of connectors with different labels. Every same-label demand connector has an unfixed hub at one or both ends. Recolor exactly one of its spokes at an unfixed hub from its main color to a different color.

Initially assign the main color to all spokes except one per private triangle, then apply the demand recolorings. The main monochromatic degree is $b(v)$ at a fixed hub and at least $b(v)-\lfloor b(v)/3\rfloor\ge3$ at every unfixed hub. A length-three connector now has distinct endpoint colors, with main degree at least three at one end. It can choose its phase to avoid the degree at the other end. Other connectors have two main-colored ends and both relevant degrees at least three. The path table then allows an extension. Pendant constraints are met by the main color. Private cycles of length at least four can use the main color on both end spokes at the hub. In a private triangle the two spoke colors are distinct. Choose the phase so that the alternate-colored spoke has internal degree one or two, whichever differs from the final alternate degree at the hub. Its main-colored spoke can take the other phase. Distinct components of the split graph have disjoint interiors, so these extensions combine into the required 3-edge coloring. $\square$

This rules out all counterexamples whose hubs are independent. A counterexample with adjacent hubs remains possible.


## Direct connectors forming a matching

Continue with the same hub reduction, allowing a direct connector to a pendant triangle. In that case the surviving hub gets the constraint forbidding monochromatic degree two, since the pendant triangle uses degree two for the bridge color. Suppose every remaining hub has $b(v)\ge4$ and the direct connectors form a matching $D$. Then three colors suffice even with these direct connectors.

Choose main labels of the hubs with distinct labels on each edge of $D$, maximizing the number of length-three connectors that cross labels. At a matched hub the label can change to the third color, so at most half of its length-three connectors have equal labels at both ends. At an unmatched hub the corresponding bound is one third. Form the multigraph of $D$ together with the same-label length-three connectors. Its degree at a matched hub is at most $1+\lfloor(b(v)-1)/2\rfloor$ and at an unmatched hub at most $\lfloor b(v)/3\rfloor$. Orient it with indegree at most the ceiling of half its degree, by adding edges pairing its odd-degree vertices, orienting Euler circuits and deleting the added edges. Each indegree is then at most $b(v)-3$.

On a direct edge use the main label of its tail. Its head reserves the incoming label for that single edge, which is possible because $D$ is a matching. On a same-label length-three connector change the label at the head to an alternate color. Use the same designated alternate color on the alternate spoke of every private triangle at that hub. If the hub has an incoming direct edge, the alternate is the third color, distinct from both its main label and that incoming label. Each incoming edge of the orientation costs at most one main spoke, so every main monochromatic degree is at least three. A direct edge now has degree at least three at its tail and one at its head. The other connectors and private structures extend by the path table as before.

## Bounded cycle rank at arbitrary order

**Computer-assisted partial theorem.** A connected noncactus simple graph of minimum degree at least two and cycle rank $m-n+1\le4$ has a locally irregular 3-edge coloring.

Suppress all degree-two paths to make a connected multigraph kernel. The hubs have degree at least three. Their mutual paths produce edges, with parallel edges allowed. Their private cycles produce loops. Suppression preserves cycle rank. If the kernel has $h$ hubs and rank $r$, its edge count is $h+r-1$, and its total degree excess over two is $2(r-1)$. Consequently $h\le2(r-1)\le6$ and the edge count is at most nine. A graph with zero or one hub is a cactus. In the remaining cases, a kernel is noncactus exactly when removing one of its nonloop edges leaves two distinct simple paths between the endpoints, counting parallel edge copies separately.

Only connector lengths 1, 2, 3 and 4 and private cycle lengths 3 and 4 are needed. The relation for length three is contained in that for every odd length at least five. The relation for length four is contained in that for every even length at least six. One may therefore shorten these paths or private cycles when searching for counterexamples. A coloring of the shortened graph supplies hub incidence colors and monochromatic degrees that extend along the original longer paths. Simplicity restricts a parallel class to at most one length-one connector.

The exhaustive scan covered 87 noncactus kernel isomorphism classes and all 1,781,720 normalized length assignments. A second implementation completed the same counts with 220,942 witnesses. It builds the path relations directly by enumerating edge-color sequences, enumerates degree-constrained multigraph kernels, and checks valid length assignments using compatible hub states. A single witness can also certify other length assignments when each of their connectors accepts its corresponding pair of incidence states. It is sufficient to check all remaining assignments and to compare the independently computed counts. [Reproducible checker](06-local-irregularity-rank-check.py)


## A uniform reduction across a two-edge cut

For a connected fragment $H$ with two marked ports, attach one uncompleted edge incidence at each port, with both incidences counted if the ports are at the same vertex. In a three-color assignment, require local irregularity on all internal edges. Record the possible terminal monochromatic degree pairs separately for equal and distinct port colors. Renaming colors leaves these two relations unchanged.

To join two fragments by two edges, choose the same color orbit on both sides. The degree pairs must differ in both coordinates. Thus two equal-orbit pairs $(a,b),(a',b')$ with $a\ne a'$ and $b\ne b'$ suffice, and the same applies in the distinct orbit. This is exact because two chosen port color patterns in the same orbit can be aligned by permuting one fragment's palette. If an orbit in one fragment contains three pairs with distinct first and distinct second coordinates, an incompatible fragment has no state in that orbit. If an orbit contains two such pairs $(a,b),(a',b')$, an incompatible fragment has at most the two cross pairs $(a,b'),(a',b)$ in that orbit. Conversely, a fragment is incompatible with its own mirror precisely when each orbit is contained in a row or in a column of the degree-pair table. If the ports are distinct, the mirror join is simple. It is noncactus if the fragment is noncactus or there are two simple paths between its terminals. In a vertex-minimal counterexample, a smaller two-edge-cut side with one of these noncactus properties consequently has a two-pair matching in at least one orbit whenever its mirror join has fewer vertices.

A rooted tree with one port has a nonempty set of possible port degrees. Attach a pendant of length one or two to get an even-sized tree and use the known three-color tree case. If the set consists of just one degree, join the tree to an identical copy by a bridge. Since the two degrees on the bridge cannot differ, the result has no 3-coloring and must be an odd path. Thus the only rigid tree attachment is a path rooted at an endpoint, and its forced degree is one or two. A flexible rooted tree can choose an external degree avoiding any single forbidden value. The same bridge argument and the cactus result rule out singleton degrees above three for rooted cactus fragments. [Tree case](https://www.labri.fr/perso/sopena/pmwiki/uploads/Publications/bbs_2013.pdf) · [Cactus case](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03941)

Consider a tree fragment with two ports. Its terminal-to-terminal path has two core incidences per vertex, counting the port stubs. Off the path, the attached branches are either flexible or forbid local degree one or two on the corresponding bridge. With at least three attached branches, all can be assigned an unused color, since the two core incidences use at most two colors. The resulting branch color degree is at least three, so this includes the undecorated local relation. For zero to two branches, direct enumeration gives the following inclusion-minimal local relations.

| Representative | Equal colors | Distinct colors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A$, undecorated | $(2,2)$ | $(1,1)$ |
| $B$, one leaf | $(3,3)$ | $(1,2),(2,1)$ |
| $C$, two length-two branches | $(2,2),(3,3),(4,4)$ | $(1,3),(3,1)$ |

To compose two relations, match the intervening port color and require unequal intervening degrees. Let $D$ be the relation of two undecorated vertices joined by an edge, and $E$ the relation of a path of three undecorated vertices. Their equal-color relations are respectively empty and $\{(1,1)\}$, and their distinct-color relations are respectively $\{(1,2),(2,1)\}$ and $\{(1,1),(2,2)\}$. The following entries are contained in the indicated series compositions. Since composition preserves inclusion, the table proves the claim for arbitrary path lengths.

| Left relation | Append $A$ | Append $B$ | Append $C$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | $D$ | $E$ | $D$ |
| $B$ | $E$ | $D$ | $E$ |
| $C$ | $D$ | $E$ | $D$ |
| $D$ | $E$ | $D$ | $E$ |
| $E$ | $D$ | $E$ | $D$ |

Hence a tree side with distinct ports in a two-edge cut can be replaced by a two-vertex edge or a three-vertex path. A tree side with coincident ports can be replaced by a single vertex, a vertex with a leaf, or a vertex with two length-two branches. These replacements preserve simplicity and noncactus status of the complete graph, whenever its two-edge join was simple and noncactus, and any coloring of the replacement extends to the original. In particular, a vertex-minimal noncactus counterexample has no such tree side above three vertices with distinct ports or above five vertices with coincident ports.


### Two-edge cuts into two cacti

**Computer-assisted partial theorem.** Let $G$ be a finite connected simple noncactus graph. Suppose deleting two edges gives exactly two connected components, both cacti, and both deleted edges join these two components. Then $G$ has a locally irregular 3-edge coloring.

#### Branches and cycle petals

An attached rooted cactus linked to a retained vertex by a bridge is represented by its possible monochromatic degrees on the attached end of the bridge, with the bridge counted at that end. This signature is nonempty. Add a pendant of length one or two to give the cactus an even number of edges. The nondecomposable exceptions and the bow-tie have odd edge count, so the resulting cactus has a 3-coloring. Restrict it to obtain a port witness. If the signature is a singleton, join two copies of the rooted piece by a bridge. This graph is a cactus and has no 3-coloring. It is the bow-tie, whose central cap forces degree three, or it belongs to the nondecomposable exceptions, whose maximum degree is at most three. Thus a branch with a singleton signature forbids a degree in $\{1,2,3\}$ at its retained end. Any signature with at least two values can avoid every prescribed degree. Such a branch may be replaced by a harder branch forbidding degree one. Palette symmetry lets this argument respect any specified color of the bridge. [Exact cactus theorem and nondecomposable exceptions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.03941)

A private cycle at a junction is attached only through that junction, which serves as both endpoints of its path of edges around the cycle. In the normalization below these cycles become triangles or squares. Each triangle uses two distinct colors at the junction and rejects a degree pair only if both degrees equal one or both equal two. Each square allows equal colors on its two spokes. For different colors it rejects only the pairs $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$. These conditions follow from the degree-two path table and the fact that two same-color spokes already give degree at least two at the junction.

#### Finite junction bounds

Suppose a junction retains $t\in\{2,3,4\}$ incidences. Other attachments consist of $p$ triangle petals, some square petals and some branches of the three forbidden-degree types. Let $F$ be the number of free color slots, namely twice the number of squares plus the number of branches. The retained incidence colors are fixed, with counts $k_i$ for the three colors. For each color $i$ there are at most $k_i$ forbidden degrees at this junction arising from the exact degrees at the other ends of its retained edges. We use the following fact about triangle petals. The three occupied slot counts can be any nonnegative $r_i\le p$ summing to $2p$, provided the used pairs meet the triangle condition. To realize the counts, choose color $i$ as the omitted color in exactly $p-r_i$ petals.

First, if $F\ge\max(t,12-2p)$, all attachments at this junction can be deleted and restored after coloring the retained graph. Choose triangle occupancy counts $r_i$ so the three $k_i+r_i$ are all at most four if their sum is at most twelve, and all at least four otherwise. In the first case the available capacities are $\min(p,4-k_i)$. Their sum is at least $\min(p,4-t)+2\min(p,4)$, by concavity in the $k_i$ and the fixed sum $t$. This bound is at least $2p$ when $t+2p\le12$, using $p\le4$ and $p>4$ separately. In the second case the lower bounds $4-k_i$ sum to $12-t\le2p$, and are individually at most four and hence at most $p$. This proves the required allocation in both cases.

Raise the provisional degrees to at least four if needed. The overhead is $\max(0,12-t-2p)$, leaving at least $t$ free slots. To avoid the forbidden-degree sets $M_1,M_2,M_3$, first choose the additional slots in color one from $0$ through $|M_1|$. Next use the choices $0$ through $|M_2|+|M_3|$ for color two, avoiding its own forbidden degrees and those for the residual count in color three. The residual is nonnegative. Now all final counts are at least four. Every triangle assignment of the chosen occupied pairs works, the square spokes can occupy any designated pairs of remaining slots, and the branches can occupy the other slots arbitrarily.

Second, sufficiently many triangle petals also let us delete and restore all attachments, for any number of free slots. The thresholds are $p=4,6,7$ at arities $t=2,3,4$ respectively. Here are the count choices.

- If all retained incidences have one color, vary its triangle occupancy from zero through $t$ to avoid its at most $t$ forbidden degrees. Give the other colors occupancies $p$ and $p-r_1$ and put all free slots in the color of occupancy $p$. Any petal using the retained color has degree at least three on that color, and any other used pair includes a color of degree at least four.
- If exactly two retained colors occur and $t\ge3$, choose their occupancies starting at $\lfloor p/2\rfloor$ and $\lceil p/2\rceil$, with increments from zero through $k_1$ and $k_2$ respectively. They can avoid all forbidden degrees separately. With $p\ge t+3$, the leftover occupancy in the unused color is between three and $p$, and the two retained colors have degrees at least four. Assign all free slots to the unused color.
- If exactly two retained colors occur and $t=2$, take each occupancy from $\{\lceil p/2\rceil,\lceil p/2\rceil+1\}$, avoiding one degree for each. The remaining occupancy is at least two, and all free slots go to its color. The only possible issue is a single free slot of forbidden type three when $p$ is four or five and the spare occupancy is two. If one lower choice is available, use it to increase spare occupancy to three. Otherwise both lower choices were forbidden, and one used occupancy can be increased by one more. This leaves spare occupancy one and final spare degree two, which the type-three branch permits. Its partners' degrees on triangle petals are at least three.
- If all three retained colors occur, assign the free slots to color one and choose omitted counts $o_1$ from zero through $k_1$ and $o_2$ from zero through $k_2+k_3$, with $o_3=p-o_1-o_2$. Avoid the forbidden-degree sets just as in the first bound. With $p\ge5$ the omissions are valid and the first two colors have degrees at least five and three respectively. Every triangle pair includes one of those colors. All free slots use the first color and meet the branch or square conditions.

When restoring several junctions, keep all retained edge colors fixed. Restore one junction at a time and avoid the currently realized degree at each retained neighbor. Subsequent restorations avoid the updated degree as well. This works even when the original boundary degrees of those neighbors are large, because only the number of forbidden values enters the proof.

#### Exact local domains and normalization of private cycles

It suffices to enumerate port relations for $p<4,6,7$ at arities two, three and four respectively, and $F<\max(t,12-2p)$. A port symbol is its color and its junction's monochromatic degree in that color. To enumerate a local domain, choose the retained port colors and the total degree counts. Count the possible allocations of triangle and square spokes by adding their allowed pairs of colors. For the remaining colored branch slots, a type-$j$ branch excludes exactly the slots in color buckets whose final degree is $j$. Any two distinct branch types together allow every slot. Consequently Hall's conditions reduce to a single inequality per type. The count of branches of each type must fit in the slots outside its forbidden buckets. This enumeration gives eight, 44 and 83 inclusion-minimal domains at arities two, three and four. The retained and attached slots sum to at most 19 in these bounded cases, so exterior path comparisons use degree 20 to represent every degree above 19.

An arc with at least one internal vertex is a series of arity-two domains. At each join, match the port color and require unequal degrees. Composition is monotone under inclusion. The checker closes the eight local relations under series composition and takes exterior relations by comparing the end symbols against the two outside incidences. It distinguishes arcs with one internal vertex from arcs with at least two internal vertices. For every exterior relation in the latter class, after symmetrizing the two positions it verifies containment of the private-triangle or private-square relation. Equal colors at a private cycle use the same monochromatic degree at both ends. Distinct colors can be placed on either spoke of the cycle without changing the junction's color-degree counts.

This is the induction that normalizes all private cycles. Root the cactus's block-cut tree at the retained component. Replace branches through bridges by the port constraints above. For a cycle joined at one vertex, normalize its descendant cycles first. There are at least two internal vertices along the arc to the same junction, so its relation contains the corresponding triangle or square relation after allowing the spoke order to be reversed. Restore any discarded large junction attachments by the degree-avoidance construction. Then lift the normalized cycles from the root toward the leaves, using the chosen colors and final degrees as boundary data. A normalized triangle or square at a fixed junction is therefore a sufficient stand-in for any original private cycle with its descendant attachments.

For a traversed arc with distinct endpoints, the two positions cannot be interchanged arbitrarily. The checker keeps the ordered relations. It obtains seven inclusion-minimal nonempty-interior connector modes and includes the direct edge as an eighth mode. Internal degrees represented by a normalized arc are bounded by the local enumeration, so the exact relation inclusion also holds for an exterior degree that exceeds them all.

#### Joining two arbitrary cactus sides

In a side with distinct ports, follow the block-cut-tree path connecting the ports. A traversed cycle gives two internally disjoint arcs between its entry and exit. There can be at most one direct edge among the two arc modes. At adjacent traversed cycles sharing a junction, its two arc incidences on each side give four retained incidences. An end of a traversed chain has two arc incidences and one bridge or external port. All other cycles are private, and bridges connecting different pieces carry one incidence. If the two ports coincide, there is no traversed chain, and all cycles are private attachments at that junction or within its branches.

The finite chain computation starts with the three-incidence end domains, retains the ordered pair of incidence symbols while passing through a shared vertex and its two preceding arcs, and ends at another three-incidence domain. It closes under any number of intermediate four-incidence junctions using inclusion-minimal relations. The execution retains 124 intermediate states after 80,919 transitions, giving 24 minimal complete traversed-component relations. The final series closure adjoins these traversed components and the eight coincident-port junction relations by bridges.

The final closure records two flags. One records whether the two terminal incidences are at the same vertex. The other records **whether the side contains two different simple paths between its terminal vertices**, which here means that the terminal route traverses a cycle. A join of the two sides is simple if at least one side has distinct terminals. In that simple join, it is noncactus exactly when at least one side has the second flag. If the side has two terminal routes, either cut edge belongs to two distinct cycles in the joined graph. If both sides have unique terminal paths, these paths consist only of bridges, and the added cycle shares no edge with any original cycle.

The final checker closes the four flag classes together by computing series compositions and retaining inclusion-minimal relations within each class. It checks compatibility for every remaining pair describing a simple noncactus join. The run checks 8,198 bridge compositions and closes with 61 minimal relations, with a compatible pair for every required join. All dominance arguments preserve the relevant flags. This verifies the theorem for unbounded cactus sizes. Run the standalone checker with Python 3. [Reproducible cactus-cut checker](06-local-irregularity-cut-check.py)


#### A size bound on cactus sides of minimal counterexamples

Each retained relation has a concrete cactus representative. A type-one branch is a single new leaf, and a type-two branch is a new path of length two. A type-three branch consists of a bridge to a new vertex with two private triangles. To see that the last branch forces three at the triangle junction, the rim edge of each triangle must match exactly one spoke. The spoke matching the rim needs a junction degree other than two, and the other spoke needs degree at least two. If the bridge color appears in neither triangle, the other two colors each have degree two and cannot provide matching spokes. If it appears once, its junction degree is two, so it is the nonmatching spoke. Its partner appears in the other triangle as well and hence has degree two, which fails on the matching spoke. If it appears in both triangles, it has degree three and both other spokes can share the same second color. These counts certify the type-three representative.

The checker counts vertices of its retained representatives, using these concrete branches, the normalized arc representatives and ordinary bridge links. It finds a maximum of 26 vertices for distinct terminals, and 16 for coincident terminals. The normalization and relation-containment steps let any cactus side be replaced by the corresponding representative. A coloring of the replacement extends back to the original graph while holding the other side fixed. Large junctions are restored with their outside incidences included among the inequalities to be preserved.

For replacing a side while the exterior graph is fixed, one can compress further. In each palette orbit map a side's relation $R$ to its acceptance relation
$$
\{(a,b):\text{ some }(x,y)\in R\text{ satisfies }x\ne a\text{ and }y\ne b\}.
$$
This map is monotone and the exterior degree sentinel still represents all degrees above 19. An exterior witness accepted by a replacement is enough, even if the replacement's own terminal degrees differ from those of the original side. Retaining acceptance-minimal realizable probes among distinct-terminal representatives gives four probes of orders $2,3,9,9$. Retaining them among all representatives gives seven probes of orders $1,2,3,5,5,8,13$. The checker confirms both calculations. As before, applying the original-side relation may require restoring deletions by degree avoidance.

**Corollary.** In a vertex-minimal counterexample to the revised conjecture, any cactus side of a genuine two-edge cut has at most nine vertices with distinct terminals, and at most 13 when its terminals coincide. Both sides cannot be cacti by the theorem, so the exterior side is noncactus. A replacement probe can always preserve simplicity. For a distinct-terminal side choose a distinct-terminal probe. If the original terminals coincide, the other side has distinct terminals and any of the seven probes may be used. The new graph stays noncactus because the exterior side already contains an edge in two cycles. A strictly smaller replacement would be 3-colorable by minimality. Its exterior coloring is accepted by the original side, giving a 3-coloring of the alleged counterexample.

If a single bridge has a cactus side and a noncactus side, the same argument gives a bound of five vertices on its cactus side in a vertex-minimal counterexample. The rooted cactus signature is nonempty, and the leaf, the two-vertex path, or the five-vertex half of the bow-tie forbids degrees one, two, or three respectively. A singleton signature uses its corresponding probe. A signature with more than one value allows every exterior degree and can use the leaf.

### A noncactus block of parallel paths

**Computer-assisted partial theorem.** Form a finite connected simple graph from $k\ge3$ pairwise internally vertex-disjoint paths having the same two distinct endpoints. Attach arbitrary rooted cacti at any vertices, each cactus meeting the rest of the graph only at its root. Then the graph has a locally irregular 3-edge coloring. Equivalently, this applies when the only noncactus block is such a union of paths.

Normalize the cactus attachments along the interiors of the $k$ paths using the previous arc computation. There are seven nondirect modes with ordered endpoint symbols. Simplicity allows at most one direct path. If $k$ is three or four, use the reduced arity-three or arity-four junction relations at the two common endpoints. Both junction domains are invariant under permutations of their port positions, so it suffices to try the multisets of connector modes, recording each connector with the same left-to-right order. This gives 112 choices for $k=3$ and 294 for $k=4$. For a pair of junction states, compute the mode choices that each of the $k$ port pairs supports and intersect these choices across the ports. The checker verifies that the union over all state pairs covers every mode multiset, for all $216{,}832$ and $2{,}025{,}366$ cases respectively.

For $k\ge5$ the checker only needs one property of the normalized arc table. Every nondirect mode accepts different port colors if one degree is at least five and the other at least four, in either order. Give the common endpoints different main colors. If a direct path is present, color it with the first endpoint's main color and count it as the second endpoint's one non-main port. All other retained ports have the main color of their own endpoint. Use that main color for all attached branches and all square spokes, and use it for one spoke of each triangle. Use one common alternate color for the other triangle spokes, avoiding the direct edge's color at its non-main end. The main degrees are at least $k$ and $k-1$ respectively. The non-main end of the direct edge has monochromatic degree one in the direct edge's color. All attachments satisfy the local conditions. The other paths extend by the stated arc property, and normalization lifts the coloring to the given graph.

In particular, the theorem covers a three-edge cut into cactus sides whenever every pair of cut terminals on each side has a unique simple connecting path. The union of these paths on either side is a tree, with a single junction for the three arms when the three port incidences are counted. Some arms may consist only of the corresponding cut incidence. Off these two trees the attachments are rooted cacti, so merging opposite arms across the cut produces precisely the three internally disjoint paths in the theorem.

### Subdivided cores of larger minimum degree

**Computer-assisted partial theorem.** Start with a finite connected loopless multigraph $H$, subdivide edges as desired to give a simple graph, and attach arbitrary rooted cacti at the resulting vertices. Let $R$ be the graph on the original vertices of $H$ whose edges were left unsubdivided. Each of the following hypotheses guarantees a locally irregular 3-edge coloring.

| Minimum degree of $H$ | Condition on $R$ |
| --- | --- |
| 6 | No edges |
| 7 | A matching |
| 10 | Each component has at most one cycle |

As above, normalize each connector with interior vertices to one of the seven nondirect modes. Call the mode represented by a bare three-edge path *strict*. Its ports of different colors always work if either monochromatic degree is at least five. Each other nondirect mode accepts every color pairing when both degrees are at least five. The checker verifies these table properties for all degrees up to the sentinel.

Choose a main color at each original vertex, giving different colors to the two endpoints of each edge of $R$. A graph whose components contain at most one cycle is properly 3-vertex-colorable, so this choice is possible. Among those choices maximize the number of strict connectors whose endpoints have different main colors. A strict connector with equal main colors is designated for recoloring at exactly one endpoint. An orientation of these connectors specifies which endpoint receives the change. An ordinary edge oriented from $u$ to $v$ keeps the main color of $u$ and uses that color as a reserved color at $v$. We always orient $R$ with at most one incoming edge at each vertex. A vertex that receives a strict connector for recoloring can use any non-main color other than its reserved color, if present.

Let $t$ be the degree at a given vertex of $H$. In the absence of direct edges at that vertex, the number of incident strict connectors designated for recoloring somewhere is at most $\lfloor t/3\rfloor$, since either of the other two main colors was available. With exactly one direct edge, it is at most $\lfloor(t-1)/2\rfloor$, since one alternative main color was available. Both observations follow by considering a change of the main color only at that vertex in the maximizing assignment.

A multigraph can be oriented with indegree at most the ceiling of half the degree at each vertex. Pair the odd-degree vertices by added edges, orient Euler tours, then remove the added edges.

- If $R$ has no edges, balance the strict connectors designated for recoloring. A vertex loses at most $\lceil\lfloor t/3\rfloor/2\rceil$ main ports, leaving at least five for $t\ge6$.
- If $R$ is a matching, balance the graph consisting of those strict connectors and the edges of $R$ together. A vertex touched by $R$ loses at most $\lceil(1+\lfloor(t-1)/2\rfloor)/2\rceil$ main ports, leaving at least five for $t\ge7$. The bound for other vertices is the same as before.
- If every component of $R$ has at most one cycle, orient its cycle edges cyclically and its hanging tree edges away from the cycle, or orient a tree away from a root. Balance the strict connectors designated for recoloring independently. A vertex with at least two direct neighbors then loses at most $1+\lceil(t-2)/2\rceil=\lceil t/2\rceil$ main ports. A vertex with one direct neighbor loses at most $1+\lceil\lfloor(t-1)/2\rfloor/2\rceil$. Either bound leaves at least five for $t\ge10$, as does the original bound for vertices without direct neighbors.

Use the main color for all attached branches, both spokes at each square, and one spoke at each triangle. Use one non-main, nonreserved color for the other triangle spokes. Every main count is at least five and every reserved count is exactly one. Thus each direct edge has distinct monochromatic degrees. A strict connector has different port colors and a main degree at least five at an endpoint. Every other connector uses main colors at both ends. All normalized constraints hold, and the coloring lifts back to the original graph.

### Further exclusions for small cuts

The five-vertex half of the bow-tie is the only rooted cactus whose one-port signature is $\{3\}$. For a singleton signature, join two copies by a bridge at their roots. The resulting cactus has no 3-coloring. If it is the bow-tie, the claimed identification follows. Otherwise it is one of the nondecomposable cacti. Its joining vertices have degree three if their signature had value three, so the only possible exception is the family built from vertex-disjoint triangles connected by odd paths and with even pendant paths at the remaining triangle positions. Every degree-three vertex of this family lies in one of its triangles. [Description of the nondecomposable family](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.03941)

For a member of this latter family, cut at a bridge incident with a triangle vertex and consider the side containing that triangle. Root its tree of triangles there. Every even pendant path forces degree two at the vertex immediately after the triangle, in the incident path edge's color. This follows by alternating the degree-two path equality bits backwards from the leaf. An odd path to a child triangle has the same effect if the child triangle's port has monochromatic degree two. For an odd path of length one the child root itself has degree two in that color, and for a longer odd path the equality bits alternate from a zero at the last internal vertex to a one at the first. Thus all third edges at the two non-root triangle vertices, when present, forbid monochromatic degree two in their own color at the triangle. This gives an induction on the tree of triangles.

For the step, write $x,y$ for the other vertices of the root triangle. If the root port had monochromatic degree three, both triangle spokes would have its color. An edge $xy$ in that color would join two color-degrees equal to two, since neither neighbor may match the root's degree three. A different color on $xy$ would have to repeat on a branch at $x$ or $y$, which forbids the resulting degree two. If the root port had degree one and the two spokes had the same color, $xy$ in the spoke color would force degree three at both $x$ and $y$, while any other color again requires a degree two forbidden by a branch. If the two spokes had distinct colors, each of $x,y$ would need $xy$ to repeat its own spoke color. An attempt to repeat a spoke on the branch alone would give forbidden degree two. Therefore the port has degree two, completing the induction.

It follows that a vertex-minimal counterexample with a bridge and one cactus side has at most two vertices on that side, unless the side is exactly the five-vertex rooted half of the bow-tie. A side with a flexible signature can use the leaf probe, and singleton degrees one and two can use the leaf and the two-vertex path respectively.

A different exclusion applies to genuine two-edge cuts whose sides are both noncactus. In a vertex-minimal counterexample, the two cut edges cannot meet at a common vertex of degree three on either side. To see this, join either side to a fresh edge, with one new cut edge incident to each endpoint of the fresh edge. Each new graph is simple and noncactus, and strictly smaller because a noncactus side has at least four vertices. At the fresh edge's endpoints the ports must have different colors. In that case their monochromatic degrees are $(1,2)$ or $(2,1)$. Thus each original side can realize a distinct-color port pair other than $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$. At a coincident terminal of degree three such a pair is either $(1,2)$ or $(2,1)$, and swapping the two incidences gives both. These two degree pairs together admit every exterior pair except $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$. Consequently the two original sides can be joined with three colors.

### Subcubic graphs without a K4 minor

**Computer-assisted partial theorem.** Every finite simple decomposable subcubic graph with no $K_4$ minor admits a locally irregular 3-edge coloring. The proof also applies to a single noncactus block that is subcubic and $K_4$-minor-free even when arbitrary rooted cacti are attached at its vertices.

The seven acceptance-minimal cactus probes, among all terminal patterns, have exactly the acceptance relations of the seven nondirect arc modes. The checker compares these sets directly. The representatives are the three bare paths of lengths two, three and four and four paths of length two with specified cactus attachments at their interior vertex. Consequently, in a two-edge separation where the exterior endpoints are distinct and the other side is a connected cactus, replacing the cactus side by the interior of one of the seven modes preserves colorability in the needed direction. Every coloring of the replacement has the same two retained incidence colors on the exterior as a coloring of the original side. Its exterior monochromatic degrees are unaltered in the lift, because each exterior vertex still has one incidence with that color. The acceptance test supplies a coloring of the normalized original side at those fixed degrees. Normalization lifts this coloring further by restoring arbitrary attachments.

First consider the claim for a single noncactus block $B$. Suppress the degree-two vertices of $B$ to obtain a connected bridgeless cubic multigraph $H$. There are no loops, since a proper cycle attached at only one branch vertex would make that vertex a cut vertex of $B$. The underlying simple graph of $H$ is a minor of $B$. In a loopless cubic multigraph with more than two vertices, three parallel edges would form a separate component. If $H$ is simple, Dirac's minimum-degree theorem gives a $K_4$ minor, so there must be two parallel edges $uv$. Let $a,b$ be the respective remaining neighbors of $u,v$. They are distinct. If they coincided, their third incidence would be a bridge out of the three-vertex set $\{a,u,v\}$. [Dirac's minimum-degree theorem](https://users.monash.edu.au/~davidwo/papers/FijWoo-EJC10.pdf)

In the subdivided realization, cut the two exterior incidences of the subgraph consisting of $u,v$, their two parallel routes, and their remaining routes up to but excluding $a,b$. The two parallel routes make one cycle and all off-route attachments are cacti meeting the routes at a vertex. The isolated side is therefore a connected cactus. Replace it by a normalized connector using the acceptance property. The new kernel is obtained by removing $u,v$ and their incident edges and inserting one edge $ab$. The underlying simple graph is a minor of the previous one, obtained by keeping one $uv$ edge and contracting the two external edges. It remains cubic, loopless and bridgeless. To see the last property, cycles avoiding the deleted piece persist, cycles passing through it shorten through $ab$, and $ab$ itself lies on the shortening of a cycle through an old edge from $a$ into the piece. The realization is simple because the new connector has an internal vertex. It is again a subdivision with only rooted cactus attachments, so the step repeats. The kernel eventually consists of two vertices joined by three parallel edges, which gives the three-path case already proved. Reverse the replacements one by one, with the exterior coloring fixed. This proves the single-block statement.

Now let the entire graph be subcubic. Nontrivial blocks share no vertex, because each would use at least two of its incidences there. Thus they are linked through bridges. If the graph is a cactus, the established cactus theorem applies and the bow-tie is excluded by subcubicity. Otherwise, choose any noncactus block as the root and process other noncactus blocks from the farthest first. After descendant replacements, each such block has only cactus attachments. Its parent bridge meets it at a vertex of degree two within the block, so the open port degree is in $\{1,2,3\}$.

Attach in turn one of the three rooted cactus probes that force degree one, two or three at the far end of that bridge. The graph of this test has just the one noncactus block, so it is covered by the preceding statement. This shows that the open side can avoid every value in $\{1,2,3\}$ at its port and therefore has at least two different admissible degrees. It can be replaced by a leaf. In a later lift its bridge color is assigned by palette symmetry and its degree is chosen to differ from the degree in the exterior coloring. Once all these replacements have been made, color the root block with its cactus attachments and lift to the original graph. The same proof works for arbitrary collections of subcubic blocks covered by the single-block result when attached to their parents by bridges at port vertices of total degree at most three.

Arbitrary rooted cacti may be attached at its vertices. Then $G$ has a locally irregular 3-edge-coloring. The degree-two suppression of the block is one of the following kernels: the theta with three or four parallel paths, $K_4$, the triangle with two doubled sides, or the square with two opposite doubled sides. **Corollary (subcubic blocks).** Let $G$ be a connected decomposable subcubic graph, and suppose every noncactus block of $G$ either has cycle rank at most three or has no $K_4$ minor. Then $G$ has a locally irregular 3-edge-coloring.

*Proof.* If $G$ is a cactus, the cactus theorem applies, and the bow-tie is not subcubic. Two nontrivial blocks of a subcubic graph cannot share a vertex. Consider a vertex of a noncactus block with an edge outside the block. It has degree two in the block, and that outside edge is a bridge. Choose a noncactus block $B$ and a bridge $e=bq$ with $b\in B$ such that the component $C$ of $G-e$ containing $B$ has no other noncactus block. Then $C$ consists of $B$ with rooted cactus attachments. The port at $b$ has monochromatic degree in $\{1,2,3\}$. Delete the side of $e$ not containing $C$. Attach in turn, at the far end of $e$, the root of the leaf, of the two-edge path and of the rooted half bow-tie. These force monochromatic degree one, two or three at that end of $e$. Each resulting test graph has the single noncactus block $B$, with cactus attachments. By the rank-three theorem or the single-block $K_4$-minor-free theorem it is 3-colorable. Hence $C$ realizes at least two port degrees. Replace $C$ by a leaf at $q$. The reduced graph still contains a noncactus block, so it is decomposable and has fewer noncactus blocks. By induction it has a coloring. Recolor a coloring of $C$ by a palette permutation so that $e$ has the same color, and choose its port degree different from the degree at $q$. Nothing else changes. The base case of one noncactus block is the single-block theorem. $\square$
## Subdivision frames for subcubic graphs (second session, 2026-11-25)

### Status of the subcubic case

The revised conjecture restricted to maximum degree three is Conjecture 6.1 of Lužar, Maceková, Rindošová, Soták, Sroková and Štorgel. They prove it for claw-free subcubic graphs, cycle permutation graphs and generalized Petersen graphs, and state that three colors remain open for general decomposable subcubic graphs. Lužar, Przybyło and Soták proved the bound four for decomposable subcubic graphs. They state that their decomposition method does not give three colors, and they report computer checks of cubic graphs through 20 vertices and of subcubic graphs with minimum degree two through 15 vertices. Searches on 2026-11-25 for a proof of the subcubic or cubic case found none. The 2-multigraph paper treats the doubled graph $2G$, which is a different problem. The bow-tie is not subcubic, so the subcubic case has no known exception. [Subcubic paper, Conjecture 6.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04649) · [Bound four for subcubic graphs](https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.02341)

### An exact reformulation

A graph satisfies $\chi'_{\mathrm{irr}}(G)\le3$ if and only if it has a spanning subgraph $T$ such that $S=G-E(T)$ is locally irregular and every component of $T$ has a locally irregular 2-edge-coloring. Take $S$ to be one color class. The two remaining classes impose conditions only inside $T$. This is the mechanism of the ring-permutation proof of Lužar et al. It is exact, but it is useful only with a family of $T$ whose 2-colorability is automatic.

### Frames

**Definition.** A *frame* of a graph $G$ is an independent set $Y$ of vertices of degree two or three, with a designated neighbor $s(y)$ for each $y\in Y$ of degree three. Let $T$ consist of the edges $yx$ with $y\in Y$ and $x\ne s(y)$. Let $S=E(G)\setminus T$ and $X=V(G)\setminus Y$. The multigraph $H$ on $X$ has one edge $e_y$ for each $y\in Y$, joining the two $T$-neighbors of $y$. The frame must satisfy two conditions.

- (F1) $S$ is locally irregular: $d_S(u)\ne d_S(v)$ for every $uv\in S$.
- (F2) The edges of $H$ have a 2-coloring in which every vertex of $H$-degree two sees both colors and every vertex of $H$-degree at least three sees one color.

A *forest frame* is a frame in which $H$ is a forest. Every forest frame satisfies (F2), by the lemma below.

In a frame every $y\in Y$ has $d_S(y)=\deg(y)-2\in\{0,1\}$. For $x\in X$, $d_S(x)$ equals the number of neighbors of $x$ in $X$ plus the number of $y$ with $s(y)=x$. Thus (F1) asks that these values differ along edges inside $X$, and that every designated vertex $s(y)$ has $d_S\ge2$.

**Lemma 1 (coloring $H$).** Let $H$ be a loopless multigraph in which every cycle, including a 2-cycle formed by parallel edges, contains an even number of vertices of degree two. Then $H$ has an edge 2-coloring as in (F2). In particular every forest has one.

*Proof.* Treat each component separately and fix a spanning tree $F$ with a root. Color the tree edges in breadth-first order. At a vertex of degree two, the child tree edge receives the color opposite to the parent edge. A root of degree two whose two edges are both tree edges gets one edge of each color. At a vertex of degree at least three, all child tree edges receive the parent color, or color $0$ at the root. A root of degree one or two whose other edge is not a tree edge colors its tree edge $0$. These rules satisfy (F2) on tree edges. Let $f=uv$ be a non-tree edge. Both $u$ and $v$ are incident with a tree edge. Let $u=w_0,\dots,w_k=v$ be the tree path with edges $g_1,\dots,g_k$. The vertex rule at $u$ forces $c(f)=c(g_1)+[\deg u=2]$ modulo two. A vertex of degree two incident with $f$ has $g_1$ as its only tree edge, and all tree edges agree at a vertex of degree at least three. Similarly $v$ forces $c(f)=c(g_k)+[\deg v=2]$. Along the path, $c(g_{i+1})=c(g_i)+[\deg w_i=2]$. The two forced values therefore agree exactly when the fundamental cycle $P+f$ has an even number of vertices of degree two. A vertex of degree at least three with several non-tree edges forces each of them to have the color of its tree edges, so all rules hold. $\square$

The tree rules force every tree color once the root edges are chosen, and the only choice at the root is a swap of the two colors. A coloring as in (F2) therefore exists exactly when this propagation is consistent. The search program uses it as an exact test of (F2).

**Lemma 2 (frame lemma).** If $G$ has a frame, then $G$ has a locally irregular 3-edge-coloring.

*Proof.* Color $S$ with color $1$. It is locally irregular by (F1). Since $Y$ is independent, every edge of $T$ joins $Y$ to $X$ and each $y\in Y$ has exactly two $T$-edges. Thus $T$ is the full subdivision of $H$, together with isolated vertices of $X$. Color both $T$-edges of $y$ with $2+c(e_y)$, where $c$ comes from (F2). In color $c'\in\{2,3\}$, every $y$ incident with that color has degree two. A vertex $x\in X$ has degree equal to the number of $H$-edges of that color at $x$. By (F2) this number is one when $\deg_H x\in\{1,2\}$ and is $\deg_H x\ge3$ otherwise. It is never two, so every edge of colors $2$ and $3$ joins different monochromatic degrees. $\square$

Lemma 2 applies to graphs of any maximum degree. A forest frame needs only the forest case of Lemma 1. The resulting 2-coloring of the full subdivision of $H$ is elementary and does not use the sketched multigraph theorem of Lužar, Przybyło and Soták.

### Frame conjecture and exhaustive evidence

**Conjecture (frames).** Every connected decomposable subcubic graph other than $K_4$ has a frame.

By Lemma 2 this conjecture implies Conjecture 6.1 of Lužar et al., because $K_4$ has an explicit locally irregular 3-edge-coloring. It is a stronger statement and is unproved.

### Partial reductions and failed strengthenings

- A forest frame extends over a pendant path $u\,a\,b$ attached at a vertex $u$. If $u\in X$, put $a\in Y$ with both edges in $T$, so $d_S(u)$ is unchanged and $H$ gains a pendant edge. If $u\in Y$, then $u$ had degree two before the attachment. Let $s(u)=a$ and keep $a,b\in X$, giving $d_S(a)=2$ and $d_S(b)=1$. For condition (F2) this simple extension fails when $u\in X$ has $H$-degree two.
- A vertex $v$ of degree two extends a forest frame of $G-v$ when both neighbors lie in $X$ in different components of $H$: put $v\in Y$. The extension is not universal, because a neighbor may lie in $Y$ or the neighbors may lie in one tree of $H$. For (F2) the added edge also changes two $H$-degrees, which can break the parity condition.
- Natural induction hypotheses for forest frames fail in small graphs. An arbitrary independent set of degree-two vertices cannot always be forced into $Y$. The smallest failure is $K_4-e$ with one degree-two vertex forced, and there are 633 failing pairs at order eight. Forcing all vertices of degree at most two into $X$ fails already for $C_4$, and for 97 graphs at order nine. Forest frames in which every designated vertex absorbs all of its $Y$-neighbors fail for random cubic graphs of orders 6, 8, 10 and 14.
- Earlier rigid schemes also fail. Claw centers can form a maximal independent set, with the remaining paths and cycles colored. Its transfer-matrix table has many infeasible attachment patterns, for example 1,794 incompatible end-and-interior patterns on paths of eight vertices. Independent claw centers can also be required to leave a forest of 2-colorable trees. Another scheme uses colorings built along a fixed Hamiltonian cycle. The forest-of-trees scheme fails for every cubic graph of order eight, including $Q_3$. The Hamiltonian scheme fails for about half of the random Hamiltonian cubic graphs. A scheme in which $T$ is a spanning union of paths with an even number of edges passed all random cubic samples through order 18. It is a special case of a forest frame.
- In a cubic graph, (F1) says exactly that every edge outside $T$ joins vertices of different $T$-degree, because $d_S=3-d_T$. The following specializations fail. (i) Frames with no $X$-vertex of $T$-degree two, which would make (F2) automatic, fail for 28 of 30 random cubic graphs at order eight and for 30 of 30 at order twelve. (ii) Parity frames, with $T$-degrees of different parity along every edge inside $X$, fail for some graphs of orders 8 and 12. (iii) A scheme requires a proper 3-coloring $(V_0,V_1,V_2)$ and a $T$ with $d_T\equiv$ color modulo three and $Y=V_2$. There (F1) and (F2) would be automatic, but it fails for 80 of the 85 cubic graphs of order 12. (iv) In matching frames every $y$ designates its partner under a perfect matching $M$, so that $T\subseteq G-M$. These fail for a positive fraction of random cubic graphs, for example 28 of 30 at order eight with up to 2000 matchings tried per graph.
- *Generalized frames.* Lemmas 1 and 2 hold when $Y$ may contain vertices of any degree, each with exactly two $T$-edges. On all connected graphs through order eight, however, they miss 389 3-colorable graphs at order eight, mostly dense ones. Frames are therefore a tool for sparse and subcubic graphs, not for the general conjecture.
- *Local repair evidence.* Edge reduction of a cubic graph deletes $uv$ and joins the two remaining neighbors of $u$ and those of $v$. Take a random frame of the reduced graph and allow changes only within distance $R$ of $\{u,v\}$. In random order-20 samples the frame of the original graph was recovered in 88 of 134 cases for $R=1$, 131 of 134 for $R=2$ and 134 of 134 for $R=3$. A proof by this route would need a finite verification over all radius-three neighborhoods together with $H$-connectivity data. That verification is far too large in the present form.
- In random cubic graphs the search found frames with $Y$ a maximal independent set and $G[X]$ an induced linear forest. On a path of $G[X]$, the values $d_S$ alternate between $2$ and $3$ at interior vertices. Each $y$ must designate exactly one neighbor. This is an exact-multiplicity assignment problem, and the obstruction to a direct proof is the lack of a counting or Hall-type argument for it. This structure is not universal either. Among all cubic graphs of orders 4 through 12, frames of this form are missing only for $K_4$ and one graph of order 10. Requiring in addition that $G[X]$ have no isolated vertex fails for 11 of the 19 cubic graphs of order 10. For fixed random partitions of this type at orders 12 through 20, between 37 and 100 percent admitted valid designations. A proof therefore has to choose the partition and cannot work with an arbitrary maximal independent set.

### Third pass on the cubic case (second session, coordinator request)

**Reduction to a designation problem.** By the theorem of Catlin and Lai, every subcubic graph without a $K_4$ subgraph is near-bipartite. Thus $V$ splits into an independent set and a set inducing a forest; see Bonamy et al., arXiv 1903.12570. Enlarge the independent set to a maximal one inside the same partition. Removing vertices from a forest leaves a forest, and maximality gives every remaining vertex a neighbor in $Y$. In a cubic graph $G[X]$ therefore has maximum degree two and is an induced linear forest. Call such a pair an *MIL partition*. Every connected cubic graph other than $K_4$ has one. The cubic frame conjecture would therefore follow if some MIL partition admits designations $s(y)$ satisfying (F1) and (F2). The designation problem has the following form. Interior vertices of a path of $G[X]$ have one $Y$-neighbor and must alternate between designated and undesignated. A path end has two $Y$-neighbors, and $2-p$ must differ from the $T$-degree of its path neighbor, where $p$ is the number of designations it receives. An isolated vertex of $G[X]$ must receive $0$, $2$ or $3$ designations. Every $y$ makes exactly one designation, and $H$ must satisfy (F2).

**Lemma 1′ (weak parity condition, proved).** Let $H$ be a loopless multigraph with maximum degree at most three. If no component of $H$ is a cycle of odd length, then the full subdivision of $H$ has a locally irregular 2-edge-coloring. Hence Lemma 2 remains true when (F2) is replaced by

- (F2w) no component of $H$ is an odd cycle.

*Proof.* Give every half-edge at a vertex of degree three the same color $0$. A *thread* is a maximal trail whose internal vertices have degree two in $H$. Each thread is colored in one of two modes. In mode M, both halves of each subdivided edge have the same color, and the colors alternate at the internal vertices. In mode D, the two halves of each subdivided edge have different colors, and both halves at an internal vertex have the same color.

Check the monochromatic degrees. In mode M a subdivision vertex has degree two in its color, while an internal vertex of the thread has degree one in each color. In mode D a subdivision vertex has degree one in each color, while an internal vertex has degree two in its color. At a vertex of degree three, the color of the incident half-edges has degree three. At a vertex of degree one, the only edge has degree one at that vertex. All adjacent degrees therefore differ, provided a thread that ends at a vertex of degree one uses mode M.

Along a thread of $L$ edges, the color at the far end differs from the color at the start by $L-1$ modulo two in mode M, and by $L$ in mode D. A thread between two vertices of degree three, possibly the same vertex, can therefore match the required color $0$ at both ends with one of the two modes. A thread with an end of degree one uses mode M and has no constraint at that end. A path component uses mode M. A cycle component uses either mode exactly when its length is even. $\square$

The search program's default $H$ test is exactly (F2w). A random check of the explicit construction passed 13,390 connected subcubic multigraphs. Rerun it with `06-handoff/lemma1prime-check.py`. This is the subcubic case of Theorem 8 of arXiv 1611.02341, whose published proof is only sketched; the argument above is complete. Every parity-only failure in the table above disappears under (F2w).

**MIL designations under (F2w): exhaustive check through order 12.** Every connected cubic graph of order at most 12 has an MIL partition with valid designations satisfying (F1) and (F2w), except $K_4$, which has no MIL partition, and the Petersen graph. Every MIL partition of maximum size is feasible, except in the Petersen graph and one graph of order 12. That graph has adjacency lists 0:123, 1:024, 2:015, 3:067, 4:168, 5:278, 6:349, 7:3510, 8:4511, 9:61011, 10:7911 and 11:8910. Some of its maximum-size MIL partitions are infeasible and others are feasible. In random cubic graphs of orders 14, 16, 18 and 20 (60 graphs each), the search enumerated up to 3,000 MIL partitions per graph and found no infeasible partition of maximum size among those enumerated. The remaining cubic problem is now the *designation lemma*: every connected cubic graph other than $K_4$ and the Petersen graph has an MIL partition, possibly of maximum size, admitting designations under (F1) and (F2w).

In the infeasible maximum partitions of the order-12 graph, the obstruction is a counting conflict. Take $Y=\{0,4,7,9\}$. Then $G[X]$ consists of the path $1\,2\,5\,8\,11\,10$ and the edge $3\,6$. For either phase of the long path, two designators remain, and their only neighbors lie on the two-vertex path or at one end of the long path. Every split gives either equal designation counts at $3$ and $6$, which violates the path condition, or a forbidden count at the long path's end.

The exchange-and-Hall route needs a rule that selects a good MIL partition, together with a theorem for a general factor problem. The problem is bipartite, each $y$ has degree exactly one, and the sets allowed at $X$-vertices have gaps, such as $\{0,2\}$ and $\{0,2,3\}$. A parity side condition is added. No rule tested was universal, and no proof was found.

**Feasibility of the reducible-configuration route.** Every configuration list for cubic graphs must contain tree-like neighborhoods, because cubic graphs of arbitrarily large girth exist. Repair after edge reduction is not universal within radius one or two. Actual reduced frames failed in 46 of 134 and 3 of 134 samples respectively. At radius three a tree-like configuration has 30 vertices to recolor and 32 boundary edges. Each boundary edge has seven exterior interface types, and the global parity or acyclicity condition on $H$ also couples the ports. An exhaustive proof would need a forall-exists dynamic program for the tree-like case. It would also need a provably complete list of short-cycle neighborhood shapes, each with its own forall-exists check. That is a substantial separate project, and it was not completed in this session.

### Fifth pass: maximality inequality and the absorption reformulation (third session)

Throughout, $G$ is a connected cubic graph and $(Y,X)$ is an MIL partition of maximum size $\lvert Y\rvert$. Call a vertex of $G[X]$ *interior* if it has two neighbours in $X$, an *end* if it has one, and *isolated* if it has none. A $K_2$-component of $G[X]$ is a component with two vertices.

**Lemma M (maximality inequality, proved).** Let $R\subseteq Y$ and let $A\subseteq X$ be independent, with $N(A)\cap Y\subseteq R$ and with every $y\in R$ having at least two neighbours in $A$. Then $\lvert A\rvert\le\lvert R\rvert$.

*Proof.* Put $Y'=(Y\setminus R)\cup A$. It is independent, because $A$ is independent and has no neighbour in $Y\setminus R$. Its complement is $(X\setminus A)\cup R$. Here $G[X\setminus A]$ is a forest, and each $y\in R$ has at most one neighbour in $X\setminus A$ and none in $R$. Adding the vertices of $R$ one at a time therefore adds vertices of degree at most one, so $G-Y'$ is a forest. Enlarge $Y'$ greedily to a maximal independent set $Y''$. Deleting vertices from a forest leaves a forest, and maximality makes $G-Y''$ a linear forest. So $Y''$ is an MIL partition with $\lvert Y''\rvert\ge\lvert Y\rvert-\lvert R\rvert+\lvert A\rvert$. Maximality of $\lvert Y\rvert$ gives the claim. $\square$

**Corollaries (proved).**

1. With $R=\{y\}$: every $y\in Y$ has at most one interior neighbour, or exactly two interior neighbours that are adjacent. In a triangle-free graph every $y$ has at most one interior neighbour.
2. Fix a phase on every path of $G[X]$, so that interior vertices alternate between *designated* ($I^+$, receiving exactly one designation) and *undesignated* ($I^-$). Each $I^+$ vertex has a unique $Y$-neighbour, which must designate it. By Corollary 1 no $y$ is adjacent to two $I^+$ vertices, so these forced designations never conflict. Call such a $y$ *bound* and every other $y$ *free*. A free $y$ has at least two neighbours that are ends or isolated vertices.
3. Each end $e$ of a $K_2$-component has a free $Y$-neighbour, whatever the phases. Triangle-freeness is not needed. Otherwise its two $Y$-neighbours $y_1,y_2$ are bound to nonadjacent $I^+$ vertices $v_1,v_2$, and $R=\{y_1,y_2\}$, $A=\{e,v_1,v_2\}$ violates Lemma M.
4. Let $K$ be a set of free vertices connected through *usable ends*, defined below, whose two $Y$-neighbours both lie in $K$. Suppose every $y\in K$ without an interior neighbour meets at least two of these ends and every $y\in K$ with an interior neighbour meets at least one. Then the number of these ends, plus the number of vertices of $K$ with an interior neighbour, is at most $\lvert K\rvert$. To see this, take $A$ to be the ends together with the $I^-$ neighbours of $K$. It is independent because usable ends are adjacent to $I^+$ vertices. In particular such a $K$ spans at most one cycle, and a spanning tree of $K$ has at most one vertex with an interior neighbour.

**The absorption reformulation (sufficiency proved).** Use phase A on every odd path $v_1\cdots v_k$ with $k\ge3$, designating $v_2,v_4,\dots,v_{k-1}$. Both ends are then *usable*: they accept $p\in\{0,1\}$. On an even path one end is usable and the other is a *W-end*, which accepts $p\in\{0,2\}$. Let $\Gamma_U$ be the multigraph on $Y$ with one edge for each usable end, joining its two $Y$-neighbours. A component of $\Gamma_U$ is *deficient* if it is a tree and all its vertices are free.

Choose a set of free vertices to be *absorbed*, subject to four rules:

- every $K_2$-component absorbs exactly one free neighbour, which designates the end adjacent to it;
- every isolated vertex absorbs $0$, $2$ or $3$ of its free neighbours;
- a W-end may absorb both of its $Y$-neighbours when both are free;
- absorbed vertices are distinct, and every deficient component contains an absorbed vertex.

Then the remaining free vertices can be oriented in $\Gamma_U$ to distinct usable ends, since every component has a cycle, a bound vertex or an absorbed vertex as a root. The result satisfies (F1). Interior vertices alternate. Usable ends receive at most one designation, W-ends $0$ or $2$, $K_2$-ends $(1,0)$ or $(0,1)$, and isolated vertices $0$, $2$ or $3$. Condition (F2w) must be checked separately.

**Status.** For triangle-free cubic graphs the designation lemma reduces to an *absorption lemma* together with (F2w). Allowing only pairs at isolated vertices and W-ends, the absorption lemma becomes a matching problem. Contract each deficient component to a vertex $d$. Join two free vertices, or a free vertex and $d$, when they are two free neighbours of one isolated vertex or the two neighbours of a W-end. Join a $K_2$-node $\kappa$ to its free neighbours. A matching covering every $d$ and every $\kappa$ is needed. By the Tutte–Gallai criterion for covering a prescribed vertex set, an obstruction is a set $S$ whose removal leaves more than $\lvert S\rvert$ odd components consisting only of $d$- and $\kappa$-vertices. Such a barrier should yield a pair $(R,A)$ violating Lemma M, or a phase change that decreases the deficient size. This step is open, and so is the separate (F2w) argument.

**Parity form of the gadget constraints (proved).** For fixed phases the (F1) designation problem is a general factor problem. Each free $y$ designates exactly one of its non-interior neighbours, and the allowed numbers of designations received are as follows.

- A usable end receives $0$ or $1$.
- A W-end receives an even number. It has exactly two $Y$-neighbours, so the possibilities are $0$ and $2$.
- An isolated vertex receives $0$, $2$ or $3$.
- A $K_2$-component $e_1e_2$ only needs $p(e_1)\ne p(e_2)$. That holds whenever $p(e_1)+p(e_2)$ is *odd*, so an odd total is sufficient. Each end has two $Y$-neighbours, so a total of $3$ always splits as $2+1$.

Thus every constraint except the usable-end capacity and the isolated-vertex gap is a parity condition. In the absorption model, requiring an odd total at each $K_2$ instead of exactly one changed nothing in the data. Through order 14 the counts are identical.

**(F2w) in practice.** Among 723 maximum partitions at orders 12 and 14, the first SAT solution of (F1) violated (F2w) for only 5 of them. Every partition still had an (F2w)-solution. An odd cycle component of $H$ consists of ends with $p=0$, linked by $Y$-vertices that designate elsewhere. A proof still has to show that such cycles can always be broken. One route is to re-orient a free vertex of the cycle onto one of its cycle ends.

**Barrier-counting sketch for the absorption lemma (not a proof).** Consider the covering-matching graph $\Pi^*$. A component $C$ of $\Pi^*-S$ that uses only deficient components and $K_2$-nodes is *closed* when all its edges stay inside $C\cup S$. Apply Lemma M to $R$, consisting of the vertices of the deficient components in $C$ and the bound neighbours of their exits. Take $A$ to consist of the tree ends, the $I^-$ neighbours, every exit gadget whose $Y$-neighbours all lie in $R$ (one end per $K_2$), and the $I^+$ neighbours of the bound vertices. Then $\lvert A\rvert-\lvert R\rvert$ comes out as follows.

- A single deficient tree on $m$ vertices whose exits are all closed: $m+1$.
- A tree of deficient components linked by isolated-vertex pairs, with the other exits closed: $\sum m_d+1$.
- When every exit is a link inside $C$: $\sum_d (m_d+q_d)/2$, where $q_d\le1$ is the number of vertices of $d$ with an interior neighbour.

Each of these values is positive, which would contradict Lemma M. So when $S=\emptyset$ no closed odd barrier should exist. Two checks are still missing. One is independence of $A$: a tree end is adjacent to an $I^+$ vertex, a W-end to an $I^-$ vertex, and $I^+$ and $I^-$ vertices may be consecutive. The other is distinctness of the bound neighbours. For $\lvert S\rvert\ge1$, the gadgets that touch $S$ must be removed from $A$, and the margin per component has to be shown to cover the $S$-vertices. This counting, together with the even-path phase rule, is the main remaining work for triangle-free graphs.

**Order 16.** With a defect cap of 2 for intermediate multigraphs, `cubic_gen.py` produced 4,059 of the 4,060 connected cubic graphs of order 16, so it is incomplete. An exhaustive order-16 run needs `CAP = 3`, which is slower. Exhaustive claims in this section therefore stop at order 14.

**A different structure observed.** The plain SAT encoder `06-handoff/lir_sat.py` finds, for every connected cubic graph of orders 8 through 14 and for random samples at orders 20, 30 and 50, a locally irregular 3-edge-colouring of a special kind. One colour class is a disjoint union of paths $P_3$, and the vertices it misses form an independent set. Both order-6 cubic graphs fail this. If the class is only required to be a star forest, every cubic graph of order at most 14 has such a colouring, including $K_4$ and Petersen. The other two classes form $T=G-E(S)$, whose degree-3, degree-2 and degree-1 vertices are the uncovered vertices, the leaves and the centres. A thread analysis characterises when $T$ is 2-colourable. Internal thread vertices must alternate between same-colour and different-colour types. A path component must have even length, and a cycle component must have length divisible by four. An odd thread between two hubs imposes a parity equation on the hub colours. This alternative was not developed further.
